An ARIMA(p,d,q) model is a linear forecasting model that predicts a univariate time series by combining autoregressive lags, differencing to remove trends, and a moving average of past errors. It suits series that can be made stationary through transformation, and we walk through a practical checklist and diagnostic routine below so you can build and check one with confidence. Building one well means confirming stationarity before fitting, reading ACF and PACF plots as a starting point rather than a final answer, splitting data chronologically for training and testing, checking residuals for leftover autocorrelation with a Ljung-Box test, and comparing candidate models with a criterion like AICc before settling on the most parsimonious one that passes diagnostics.

## What AR, I, and MA each contribute to the model

Each letter in ARIMA(p,d,q) names a distinct mechanism, and understanding what each one does helps you read a model's output instead of treating it as a black box.

- **AR (p)**: the autoregressive term predicts the current value from a weighted combination of its own past values, where p sets how many lags enter the equation.
- **I (d)**: the integration term applies differencing, subtracting each observation from the one before it, to strip out trends and bring the mean back to a stable level; d=1 removes a linear trend, while d=2 (differencing the differences) handles a trend that is itself changing.
- **MA (q)**: the moving-average term predicts the current value using the errors from past forecasts, letting the model correct for recent surprises rather than just recent levels.

Combined, these pieces let a single equation capture momentum, drift, and short-lived shocks in one pass, which is why [stationarity and identification steps](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section4/pmc446.htm) treat the three components as building blocks to assemble in sequence rather than as competing choices.

## How to tell if your series is stationary and fix it if not

Stationarity means the mean, variance, and autocorrelation structure of a series stay roughly constant over time rather than drifting or swinging with the calendar. A series with a rising trend or a seasonal wobble violates this, and ARIMA's AR and MA terms assume the pattern they are modeling will not shift under them.

1. Plot the raw series first: a visible upward or downward trend, or a variance that widens over time, is a strong sign of nonstationarity.
2. Check the autocorrelation function: when the ACF decays very slowly across many lags instead of dropping off quickly, the series is likely nonstationary.
3. Run a unit-root test such as the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) alongside the KPSS test, since the two check opposite null hypotheses and using them together catches cases either test alone would miss.
4. Difference the series once, recheck the plot and ACF, and stop as soon as the pattern looks stable rather than differencing repeatedly out of caution.

**Overdifferencing is a real risk**: differencing a series that is already stationary introduces artificial moving-average structure and inflates the variance. A simple check is to compare the variance before and after an additional difference. If it increases, you have gone a step too far, a caution that the [Box-Jenkins methodology](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/research/population-health-methods/box-jenkins-methodology) states directly.

## Reading ACF and PACF to pick starting values for p and q

The autocorrelation function (ACF) shows how a value correlates with its own past values across increasing lags, including indirect effects carried through intermediate lags. The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) strips out those indirect effects, showing only the direct relationship at each lag. Because they isolate different kinds of dependence, reading them together narrows down reasonable starting orders before you fit anything.

## The Box-Jenkins workflow for building an ARIMA model

The Box-Jenkins approach organizes ARIMA modeling into four stages, and following them in order avoids the common mistake of jumping straight to a model fit before the data is ready.

1. **Identify**: settle on the differencing order d using the stationarity checks above, then use ACF and PACF on the differenced series to propose candidate values for p and q.
2. **Estimate**: fit the candidate model using maximum likelihood estimation, then examine the coefficient signs, sizes, and standard errors for anything implausible.
3. **Check**: compare competing models with information criteria. **AICc is generally preferred over AIC in small samples** because it corrects for the extra bias that creeps in with fewer observations, while BIC penalizes complexity more heavily. Models must share the same value of d before you compare their [AIC or AICc scores](https://otexts.com/fpppy/09-arima.html), since differencing changes the likelihood calculation itself.
4. **Forecast**: once residual diagnostics pass, generate forecasts and prediction intervals, returning to step one if the diagnostics flag structure the model missed.

This identify-estimate-check-forecast cycle is iterative by design. A failed diagnostic check is a normal part of the process, not a sign you picked the wrong method.

## What a fitted model gives you and how to read a forecast

Fitting an ARIMA model returns the estimated coefficients for each AR and MA term, an estimate of residual variance (often labeled sigma2), and a log-likelihood value used to compute the information criteria from the previous section. Checking the coefficients against your ACF and PACF expectations is a quick sanity check before moving on.

- **predict()** typically covers both in-sample and out-of-sample points, useful for checking fit against historical data.
- **forecast()** is built specifically for out-of-sample projection beyond the end of the series.
- **get_forecast()** in [common software implementations](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/examples/notebooks/generated/autoregressive_integrated_moving_average.html) returns a richer object that includes prediction intervals, which is usually what you want for communicating uncertainty.

Prediction intervals widen as the forecast horizon extends, reflecting the compounding uncertainty of projecting further into the future on a model that only has information up to the present. A narrow interval ten periods out is often a red flag rather than good news. ## Residual checks and the mistakes that trip up beginners

A well-fit ARIMA model leaves residuals that look like white noise: no remaining pattern, no significant autocorrelation, and no trend against the fitted values. Two checks confirm this directly: plotting the residual ACF to look for leftover structure, and running a Ljung-Box test on the residuals, which formally tests whether the autocorrelations as a group are statistically indistinguishable from noise.

- **Overdifferencing**: applying one more difference than needed, which adds spurious MA structure and inflates variance.
- **Random train/test splits**: shuffling time-ordered data breaks the sequence a forecasting model depends on; always split chronologically.
- **Overfitting p and q**: adding lags until the residuals look clean on the training data, which usually hurts out-of-sample accuracy.

These are among the most frequent beginner errors in ARIMA work, and each has a cheap fix: check stationarity before fitting, split by date, and favor the simplest model that passes diagnostics. **When residuals still show autocorrelation after reasonable tuning**, or the series clearly carries seasonal or <Link to={routes.article('data-drift-detection')}>production-level drift</Link> that keeps reappearing, it is worth considering SARIMA, a vector autoregression, or a machine-learning approach instead of forcing a plain ARIMA to fit.

## Where ARIMA fits among forecasting tools

ARIMA works well for interpretable, linear, single-series problems once stationarity is handled. Seasonal data calls for [SARIMA rather than inflating p or q artificially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARIMA), while nonlinear patterns or multiple interacting series point toward machine learning or vector autoregression. A simple, validated model usually beats a complex one that overfits the training window.
