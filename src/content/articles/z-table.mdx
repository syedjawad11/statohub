A **z score table** is a printed grid of pre-calculated probabilities for the standard normal distribution — the bell curve with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Search for the same grid and you will land on it under half a dozen names: a z-score table, a standard normal z-table, a normal distribution table, a z normal distribution table, a table of z-score values, or occasionally "z table normal" typed straight into a search box. They are all the same table of normal distribution probabilities, just labeled differently by whichever textbook printed them. The one skill that actually matters is reading the correct cell: matching the right row to the right column, knowing which tail the number describes, and not losing the sign on a negative z. This guide walks through that mechanics in detail, flags where even careful readers get burned, and ends with a calculator that gives you the exact value without rounding to the nearest table entry.

## What Is a Z Score Table?

A z score table is a lookup grid built for one specific distribution: the standard normal distribution, written N(0, 1), with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Every normal distribution table — cumulative or otherwise — describes that one curve, because [NIST's e-Handbook of Statistical Methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3661.htm) defines the standard normal as the case where the general normal density's location parameter μ equals 0 and its scale parameter σ equals 1.

The reason a printed grid exists at all is that the standard normal's cumulative distribution function has no closed-form algebraic solution — it has to be computed numerically, integral by integral, point by point. Before cheap computing, someone had to do that numerical work once and publish the results, which is exactly what every distribution z table and normal dist z table in a stats textbook is: a pre-computed answer key so students and analysts did not have to integrate by hand.

A table cell always holds a probability tied to a specific z: in the standard cumulative format, that is P(Z ≤ z), read "the probability that a standard normal variable Z is at or below z." For z = 1.96, that probability is 0.9750 — 97.5% of the curve sits at or below that point. The underlying bell-curve shape, the 68-95-99.7 empirical rule, and the mean/standard-deviation formula that produces a z-score in the first place are covered in full in the <Link to={routes.article('normal-distribution')}>normal distribution guide</Link>; this page picks up right where that one hands off — at the table itself.

## How Do You Read a Z Score Table?

You read a z score table by splitting your z-value into two parts and finding where they cross. The row carries the whole number plus the first decimal place; the column carries the second decimal place; the cell where they meet is the cumulative probability.

Walk through one in full. Suppose adult heights in a population are normally distributed with mean μ = 70 inches and standard deviation σ = 3 inches, and you want the share of people shorter than 75.88 inches.

```
z = (75.88 − 70) / 3 = 5.88 / 3 = 1.96
```

1. Locate the row labeled **1.9**.
2. Move across to the column labeled **0.06**.
3. Read the cell: **0.9750**.

That means 97.5% of this population is shorter than 75.88 inches — and only 2.5% is taller. The same lookup works for a table of z-score values printed in any format, as long as you know whether the table gives you a left-tail or a 0-to-z number (the next two sections cover both).

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Before trusting a cell, add the row label to the column label. Row 1.9 + column 0.06 = 1.96. If that sum does not match the z you were trying to look up, you have the wrong row or column — this one check catches the single most common table-reading mistake before it costs you an answer.
</Callout>

## How Do You Find Right-Tail and Between-Two-Z Probabilities?

Most z score tables default to the left tail, so anything else requires one extra step of arithmetic. For the area *above* a value, subtract the left-tail value from 1, because the total area under the standard normal curve equals 1:

```
P(Z > z) = 1 − P(Z ≤ z)
```

For z = 1.96, that is 1 − 0.9750 = 0.0250 — the 2.5% right-tail region that forms each tail of a two-tailed test at α = 0.05, since 0.05 split evenly leaves 0.025 on either side.

For the area *between* two z-scores, subtract the smaller cumulative probability from the larger:

```
P(z₁ ≤ Z ≤ z₂) = P(Z ≤ z₂) − P(Z ≤ z₁)
```

Between z = 0.50 and z = 1.50: P(Z ≤ 1.50) − P(Z ≤ 0.50) = 0.9332 − 0.6915 = **0.2417**. About 24.17% of the standard normal distribution sits between those two points — two lookups and a subtraction is the entire method.

A special case of that same formula handles symmetric two-tail probabilities, using the curve's symmetry about 0:

```
P(−z ≤ Z ≤ z) = 2 × P(Z ≤ z) − 1
```

For z = 1.96: 2 × 0.9750 − 1 = **0.9500**. Exactly 95% of the standard normal distribution falls within ±1.96 — the fact behind the standard 95% two-sided confidence interval, which [NIST describes as bracketing the true population parameter in about 95% of repeated samples at that confidence level](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc14.htm). The <Link to={routes.article('confidence-interval')}>confidence interval guide</Link> covers that construction in depth.

## How Do You Look Up a Negative Z-Score?

You read a negative z-score exactly the way you read a positive one — find the negative row, then the matching column — but it is where most lookup errors actually happen, because it is easy to drop the sign without noticing.

Take z = −1.25: row **−1.2**, column **0.05**, cell **0.1056**. About 10.6% of the standard normal distribution lies at or below −1.25, putting that value near the 11th percentile.

If your particular normal distribution table only lists positive z-values, symmetry gives you the negative-side answer without a second table:

```
P(Z ≤ −z) = 1 − P(Z ≤ z)
```

This holds because the curve is a perfect mirror image around 0: the area below −z equals the area above +z, which is one minus the area below +z. It is also why a symmetric two-tailed test only ever requires looking up the positive critical value — the negative one is already implied.

<Callout variant="note" title="Common slip">
Looking up z = 1.25 by habit when you actually have z = −1.25 swaps 0.8944 for the correct 0.1056 — the 89th percentile instead of the 11th. The sign is not decoration; it changes which end of the distribution you are describing.
</Callout>

## What's the Difference Between a Cumulative Z-Table and a 0-to-Z Table?

Not every z score table reports the same number for the same z, and the two formats in circulation are easy to confuse because they share the same row-and-column layout.

  ]}
/>

That second row is not a rare exception — [NIST's own cumulative distribution function table for the standard normal distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3671.htm) is published in the 0-to-z format. NIST's worked example looks up z = 1.53 by reading row **1.5**, column **0.03**, for a table value of **0.43699** — the area between 0 and 1.53, not the full area below it. Converting that 0-to-z value to the cumulative probability used throughout this page just means adding 0.5, since everything below 0 already accounts for half the curve:

```
Left-tail value = 0.5000 + body value
0.5000 + 0.43699 = 0.93699
```

NIST's own page uses the same addition for a between-two-points example: P(−1 ≤ Z ≤ 0.5) = (0.5 + 0.19146) − (1 − (0.5 + 0.34134)) = 0.69146 − 0.15866 = 0.5328, matching the between-z method from the previous section. Whichever normal distribution tables you are handed, check the heading or footnote before trusting a cell — for a positive z, a 0-to-z reading used as if it were cumulative lands exactly 0.5 away from the right answer (for a negative z, the cumulative value is 0.5 minus the body value instead).

## When Should You Use a T-Table Instead of a Z-Table?

Use a t-table, not a z score table, whenever the standard deviation in your z-formula is estimated from your own sample rather than known from an outside, trusted source. The z = (x − μ) / σ formula assumes σ is a true population parameter; plugging in a sample standard deviation s and reading a z-table understates how uncertain that estimate really is.

[NIST's guidance on confidence limits for the mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm) builds its interval directly around this distinction, using the t-distribution with N − 1 degrees of freedom whenever the sample standard deviation s stands in for an unknown σ. The common rule of thumb is to lean on the t-distribution whenever n is small (textbooks commonly cite n < 30 as the threshold worth worrying about), because the t-distribution has heavier tails that widen the interval to compensate for the extra uncertainty in s.

That gap closes as the sample grows. [NIST's table of critical values for the Student's t-distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3672.htm) lists a row labeled "Normal Values" showing 1.282, 1.645, 1.960, 2.326, 2.576, and 3.090 — the exact z-table critical values this page already uses — because as degrees of freedom climb toward infinity, the t-distribution's critical values converge on the standard normal's. By around 100 degrees of freedom the two are already nearly identical, which is why some software defaults to the t-distribution even for large samples: it is never meaningfully wrong, only slightly more conservative. For the full walkthrough of when each test applies, see <Link to={routes.article('t-test-vs-z-test')}>the t-test vs. z-test comparison</Link> and the dedicated <Link to={routes.article('t-test')}>t-test guide</Link>.

## How Do You Get These Probabilities in Excel, Python, or R Instead of a Table?

Every major tool replaces the printed grid with a single function call, and all three return the cumulative left-tail probability by default — the same number this page's table does.

```
Excel:  =NORM.S.DIST(1.96, TRUE)        -> 0.97500
Python: from scipy.stats import norm
        norm.cdf(1.96)                 -> 0.97500
R:      pnorm(1.96)                    -> 0.97500
```

Excel's [NORM.S.DIST function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/norm-s-dist-function-1e787282-3832-4520-a9ae-bd2a8d99ba88) takes the z-value and a logical `cumulative` argument; set it to TRUE for the cumulative distribution function, the direct replacement for a table lookup. Python's [SciPy `scipy.stats.norm`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.norm.html) object exposes the same cumulative distribution function through its `.cdf()` method, with no table or interpolation involved. R's [`pnorm()` function](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/Normal.html) defaults to `mean = 0` and `sd = 1` — the standard normal — and accepts a `lower.tail = FALSE` argument that returns the right-tail probability directly, skipping the 1 − p subtraction a printed table always requires.

## A Worked Example: Checking a Machined Part Against a Spec Limit

A machine shop cuts steel shafts meant to measure 25.00 mm in diameter. The process is stable and normally distributed, with mean μ = 25.00 mm and standard deviation σ = 0.08 mm, typical repeatability for this kind of CNC work. An inspector measures one shaft at 24.90 mm. Is that a sign the process has drifted?

    ]}
    direction="horizontal"

1. **Standardize the reading.** z = (24.90 − 25.00) / 0.08 = −0.10 / 0.08 = **−1.25**.
2. **Look up z = −1.25.** Row −1.2, column 0.05 → **0.1056**.
3. **Interpret it.** About 10.56% of shafts from this process run this small or smaller — roughly the 11th percentile. On its own, that is an unremarkable reading from a process behaving normally, not evidence of drift.

Now suppose the part has a hard lower spec limit of 24.84 mm — anything smaller gets scrapped. What fraction of total output falls below that line?

```
z = (24.84 − 25.00) / 0.08 = −0.16 / 0.08 = −2.00
```

Row −2.0, column 0.00 → **0.0228**.

4. **Decide.** About 2.28% of shafts from this stable process will land outside the lower spec limit by chance alone, with nothing wrong on the machine — a scrap rate worth flagging to engineering before it becomes a customer complaint. That is the real payoff of the lookup: it turns one measurement into a statement about the whole production run.

## Common Mistakes When Reading a Z Score Table

- **Mixing up rows and columns.** Reading the column as the tenths digit instead of the hundredths digit turns row 1.9, column 0.06 (z = 1.96) into row 1.6, column 0.09 (z = 1.69) — a completely different value. Fix it by checking that row plus column always equals your target z.
- **Forgetting the table gives left-tail values.** Asked for the probability *above* a point, skipping the 1 − P(Z ≤ z) step turns a correct 0.0250 right-tail answer into an incorrect 0.9750. Fix it by always asking "which side of the line do I actually need?" before reading off a cell.
- **Treating a 0-to-z table as cumulative.** A body-format cell of 0.43699 is not the probability of falling below that z — it is 0.5 short. Fix it by checking the table's heading or footnote for its format before trusting any number from it.
- **Losing the sign on a negative z.** Looking up z = 1.25 when the real value is z = −1.25 swaps the 11th percentile for the 89th. Fix it by writing the sign down before you open the table, not after.
- **Rounding the z-score too early.** Rounding to one decimal place instead of two moves the lookup further than it looks — P(Z ≤ 2.00) = 0.9772 versus P(Z ≤ 1.96) = 0.9750, a difference of about 0.002 from dropping just the hundredths digit. Fix it by keeping two decimal places through the lookup, or using the calculator above for exact precision.
- **Applying the table to non-normal data.** Standardizing a strongly skewed variable — hold times, income, reaction times — and reading the result off a z score table produces a confident-looking number that does not describe the real distribution. Fix it by checking a histogram or <Link to={routes.article('skewed-distribution')}>a skewed-distribution diagnosis</Link> before you standardize anything.

## Statohub's Take on the Z Score Table

Statohub's position is that the z score table is worth learning by hand once, even though almost nobody looks one up by hand anymore. Working a few lookups the slow way builds an intuition for how probability concentrates around the mean that a calculator's instant answer does not teach on its own. Once that intuition is in place, reach for the calculator above or a software function for anything that actually matters — the two-decimal rounding a printed grid forces is fine for a classroom exercise and not fine for a real decision.
