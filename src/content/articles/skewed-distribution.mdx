A **left skewed distribution** is one whose tail stretches out toward the lower values while most of the data piles up near the upper end of the scale. That asymmetry gives the distribution a negative skewness coefficient and pulls the mean below the median, which is the opposite pattern from a right-skewed distribution, where a long tail of high values pulls the mean upward. The direction of the tail — not where the bulk of the data sits — is what the word "left" or "right" describes. Knowing how to read that tail from a histogram, a box plot, or a simple mean-versus-median comparison tells you whether the mean or the median better represents a "typical" value in your data.

## What Is a Left-Skewed Distribution?

A **left-skewed distribution**, also called a negatively skewed distribution, has its tail on the left side of the number line — the low end — while most observations cluster near the upper end of the scale. A small number of unusually low values stretch that tail out and drag the mean down with them.

The terms **distribution skewed left** and **skewed left distribution** describe the same shape as left-skewed distribution; all three phrases show up interchangeably in textbooks and software output.

The cardinal rule for reading skew: the tail tells you the direction, not the hump. If the long, thin side of the histogram points left, the distribution is left-skewed, even when most of the bars sit on the right. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, section 2.6 on skewness and the mean, median, and mode](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-6-skewness-and-the-mean-median-and-mode), illustrates this with a small data set — 4, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8 — where the mean (6.3) sits below the median (6.5), which sits below the mode (7): "A distribution of this type is called skewed to the left because it is pulled out to the left."

That ordering usually holds for unimodal data, as a rule of thumb rather than a guarantee:

```
Mode ≥ Median ≥ Mean
```

**Common real-world examples.** An easy exam produces a left-skewed score distribution: most students land in the 80s and 90s, while a handful who struggled pull a thin tail toward the 40s and 50s. Age at death in a population with long life expectancy behaves the same way — most people live into their 70s and 80s, but a smaller number die young, creating a tail on the low side. Age at retirement follows a similar pattern: most workers retire in their early-to-mid 60s, while a smaller group retires much earlier because of disability or job loss, pulling a thin tail toward younger ages.

## How Does a Right-Skewed Distribution Differ?

A **right-skewed distribution** — also called positively skewed — has its tail on the right side, the high end. Most values sit low on the scale, and a small number of very large values stretch the tail rightward, pulling the mean up past the median:

```
Mean ≥ Median ≥ Mode
```

Household income is the textbook case. Most households earn modest to moderate amounts, while a comparatively small number of very high earners stretch the distribution's right tail out far beyond where most households sit. That is why official income reporting leans on the median rather than the mean: the [U.S. Census Bureau's *Income in the United States: 2022* report](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2023/demo/p60-279.html) headlines "real median household income," not mean income, as its primary figure. City populations follow the same pattern — thousands of small towns and a handful of megacities — and so do insurance claim amounts, where most claims are small and a few catastrophic ones pull the tail far to the right.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Whenever you see a government or financial report lead with a median instead of a mean — household income, home prices, time-to-sale — treat that as a signal the underlying distribution is right-skewed. Agencies choose the median precisely because the mean would be distorted by a long right tail.
</Callout>

## What Changes in a Left vs Right-Skewed Distribution?

A **left vs right-skewed distribution** comparison comes down to three reversals: which side the tail points, whether the mean sits above or below the median, and the sign of the skewness coefficient. Everything else about reading and handling skewed data applies symmetrically to both directions.

## How Do You Measure Skewness?

Skewness has a visual definition (which way the tail goes) and a precise numerical one. [NIST/SEMATECH's e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section 1.3.5.11 on measures of skewness and kurtosis](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35b.htm), defines skewness as "a measure of symmetry, or more precisely, the lack of symmetry," and documents two formulas worth knowing.

**Pearson's second skewness coefficient** is a fast approximation built from three summary statistics you likely already have:

```
Sₖ₂ = 3 × (mean − median) / standard deviation
```

It is negative whenever the mean sits below the median (left skew) and positive whenever the mean sits above it (right skew).

**The Fisher-Pearson coefficient of skewness** uses the full data set rather than just three summary numbers:

```
g₁ = [ Σ(Yᵢ − Ȳ)³ / N ] / s³
```

where `Ȳ` is the mean, `N` is the number of observations, and `s` is the standard deviation computed with `N` in the denominator rather than `N − 1`. NIST notes that most statistical software actually reports an **adjusted** version, `G₁`, which multiplies `g₁` by a sample-size correction factor that approaches 1 as `N` grows — 1.19 at N = 10, 1.05 at N = 30, and 1.02 at N = 100.

Software exposes this formula directly: Python's [SciPy `scipy.stats.skew` function](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.skew.html) returns `g₁` by default, Excel's [`SKEW` function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/skew-function-bdf49d86-b1ef-4804-a046-28eaea69c9fa) returns the adjusted `G₁`, and R's [`moments` package documentation](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/moments/moments.pdf) describes a `skewness()` function that, by default, returns the unadjusted `g₁` — matching SciPy's default output. All three agree on the sign: negative means left-skewed, positive means right-skewed, and values near zero suggest approximate symmetry.

## What Is Bimodality, and How Does It Relate to Skewness?

A **bimodality distribution** — more commonly written as a bimodal distribution — has two distinct peaks instead of one. Bimodality and skewness are separate properties of a distribution's shape, though they can show up together.

Picture a group where half the members are beginners and half are experts on some skills test. Their scores might cluster into two humps — one in the low 50s, one in the high 80s — which is a bimodal shape. If the beginner hump is shorter than the expert hump, the combined distribution can also carry a left skew, because the thinner, lower peak behaves like an extended tail relative to the taller one. NIST's skewness and kurtosis reference makes this interaction explicit: "If the data are multi-modal, then this may affect the sign of the skewness."

In a genuinely bimodal distribution, the usual mean-median-mode ordering stops being reliable, because the mean often lands in the valley between the two peaks — a region with little or no actual data. That is one reason analysts check a histogram before trusting any single summary statistic: a bimodal shape usually signals that two distinct subpopulations have been mixed together, and separating them often tells a clearer story than any one skewness number.

<Callout variant="note" title="Bimodal does not mean skewed">
Two symmetric peaks positioned at equal distances from the overall center can produce a bimodal distribution with a skewness coefficient near zero. A distribution can be bimodal and symmetric, bimodal and skewed, unimodal and skewed, or unimodal and symmetric — the two properties vary independently.
</Callout>

## How Do You Identify a Skewed Distribution from a Histogram or Box Plot?

**The histogram.** [NIST/SEMATECH's e-Handbook entry on the histogram](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/histogra.htm) lists skewness explicitly among the features a histogram reveals, alongside center, spread, outliers, and "the presence of multiple modes." Draw bars across evenly sized bins: if the bars taper off into a long, flat run on the left, the distribution is left-skewed; if that taper runs to the right, it is right-skewed. Choose enough bins to reveal the shape without letting bin-to-bin noise create a false second peak.

**The box plot.** A box plot shows the five-number summary — minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, maximum — built from the quartile and median definitions in [NIST's box plot reference](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/boxplot.htm). In a left-skewed distribution:

- The median line inside the box sits closer to the upper (right) edge of the box.
- The left whisker runs noticeably longer than the right whisker.
- Any flagged outliers tend to appear on the low (left) side.

A box plot's asymmetric whiskers are a fast skew signal even without a full histogram, though a box plot alone cannot reveal bimodality — a second peak is invisible once the data collapse into five summary values.

## Why Does Skewness Direction Matter for Choosing Statistical Methods?

**Choosing a measure of center.** Reporting the mean of a left-skewed distribution without flagging the skew lets readers overestimate the typical value, because the mean sits below where most of the data actually lives. The median does not share that bias.

**Choosing a statistical test.** Classical tests — t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation — assume roughly symmetric, normally distributed data. Strongly skewed data can distort their p-values; non-parametric alternatives are built to tolerate that asymmetry. The <Link to={routes.article('nonparametric-tests')}>guide to nonparametric tests</Link> covers the Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests that substitute for their parametric counterparts when skew is severe.

**Transforming data.** Analysts often transform skewed variables before running a parametric analysis — a log transform compresses right-skewed data toward symmetry, while a power or square transform can help with left-skewed data. The <Link to={routes.article('transform-skewed-data')}>guide to transforming skewed data</Link> walks through five methods, including the Box-Cox and Yeo-Johnson power transforms, and how to validate that a transform worked.

**Interpreting risk.** In finance and insurance, left-skewed returns are the riskier shape to misread: frequent small gains punctuated by rare, large losses, so the downside tail is longer than the upside. Right-skewed returns reverse that pattern: frequent small losses punctuated by rare, large gains, with the longer tail on the upside. Which direction a return series leans changes how risk gets modeled and reported.

## A Worked Example: Computing Skewness for an Exam-Score Dataset

**Setup.** Ten students take an easy exam. Their scores: 55, 75, 82, 87, 90, 91, 92, 93, 95, 95. Most scores sit high on the scale, but two scores (55 and 75) sit noticeably lower. The question: does the skewness coefficient confirm what the histogram suggests?

1. **Calculate the mean.**

```
55 + 75 + 82 + 87 + 90 + 91 + 92 + 93 + 95 + 95 = 855
mean (x̄) = 855 / 10 = 85.5
```

2. **Find the median.** With n = 10 (even), the median is the average of the 5th and 6th sorted values:

```
Median = (90 + 91) / 2 = 90.5
```

The median (90.5) already sits well above the mean (85.5) — the first sign of a left skew.

3. **Identify the mode.** The value 95 appears twice; every other value appears once, so Mode = **95**. The ordering Mode (95) > Median (90.5) > Mean (85.5) matches the classic left-skewed pattern.

4. **Calculate the standard deviation.** Compute each score's squared deviation from the mean (x̄ = 85.5):

<DataTable
  caption="Squared deviations from the mean for the exam-score dataset"
  headers={['xᵢ', 'xᵢ − 85.5', '(xᵢ − 85.5)²']}
  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[
    ['55', '−30.5', '930.25'],
    ['75', '−10.5', '110.25'],
    ['82', '−3.5', '12.25'],
    ['87', '1.5', '2.25'],
    ['90', '4.5', '20.25'],
    ['91', '5.5', '30.25'],
    ['92', '6.5', '42.25'],
    ['93', '7.5', '56.25'],
    ['95', '9.5', '90.25'],
    ['95', '9.5', '90.25'],
    ['Sum', '', '1,384.5'],
  ]}
/>

```
s² = 1,384.5 / (10 − 1) = 153.83
s = √153.83 ≈ 12.40
```

5. **Apply Pearson's second skewness coefficient.**

```
Sₖ₂ = 3 × (mean − median) / s
    = 3 × (85.5 − 90.5) / 12.40
    = 3 × (−5) / 12.40
    = −15 / 12.40
    ≈ −1.21
```

**Decision.** Pearson's second coefficient comes out to **−1.21**, confirming a left-skewed distribution: the negative sign shows the tail points left, so the median (90.5) — not the mean (85.5) — is the more honest description of a "typical" score on this test. Running the same ten scores through the Fisher-Pearson formula gives `g₁ ≈ −1.65`, which is what SciPy's `skew()` returns by default; the sample-adjusted `G₁ ≈ −1.96` is what Excel's `SKEW` function would return for this data. The three numbers differ because they use different denominators and corrections, but every one of them agrees on the direction — negative, confirming the left skew.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
A skewness coefficient's sign tells you the direction; its magnitude is only meaningful relative to other coefficients computed the same way. Don't compare a Pearson's second coefficient on one dataset to a Fisher-Pearson g₁ on another and draw conclusions from the difference in size — compare like with like, or just track the sign and the size of the mean-versus-median gap.
</Callout>

## Common Mistakes When Working with Skewed Distributions

- **Confusing tail direction with data location.** Saying a distribution is "skewed right" because most values sit on the right is backward — the skew name follows the tail, not the bulk. If most values sit right and a thin tail pulls left, the distribution is left-skewed.
- **Reporting the mean as "typical" without checking skew.** The mean is a fine measure of center for symmetric data but misleading for a left-skewed distribution; plot the data and compare mean to median before deciding which one to report.
- **Treating skewness as an error to fix.** Skewness is a property of the data, not a mistake. Exam scores on an easy test are genuinely left-skewed because most students understood the material; dropping the low scores to force symmetry introduces bias instead of removing it.
- **Assuming any bimodal distribution must be skewed.** A bimodality distribution with two symmetric, equally sized peaks can have a skewness coefficient near zero. Modality and skewness are separate properties — check both.
- **Trusting a box plot to catch a second peak.** Box plots compress a distribution into five numbers and will not reveal bimodality on their own; pair them with a histogram when you suspect more than one underlying group.

## Statohub's Take on Skewed Distributions

Statohub's position is that skewness deserves a two-second check before any mean gets reported: glance at a histogram, compare the mean to the median, and only then decide whether the mean, the median, or both belong in the write-up. That habit costs almost nothing and prevents the single most common misreading of summary statistics — presenting a mean as "typical" when a handful of tail values have quietly dragged it away from where the data actually sits.
