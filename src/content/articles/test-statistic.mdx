A **test statistic** is the single number a hypothesis test produces by dividing how far a sample result falls from the null hypothesis by the standard error of that result. Every hypothesis test boils down to one of these — a t-statistic, a z-statistic, an F-statistic, or a chi-square statistic — and once you have it, you compare it against a critical value from the matching distribution to decide whether the result is surprising enough to reject the null hypothesis. This guide walks through the general formula for a test statistic, how to find degrees of freedom for the tests you will meet most often, how to read a t distribution table, and a fully solved numeric example.

## What Is a Test Statistic?

A test statistic is a single standardized number that tells you how far your sample result sits from what the null hypothesis predicts, measured in standard-error units rather than in the original units of the data. The general test statistic formula is:

```
test statistic = (observed result − value expected under H0) / standard error
```

A test statistic of 0 means the sample landed exactly on the null hypothesis's prediction. A test statistic of 2 means the result is two standard errors above that prediction; −2 means two standard errors below. The larger the absolute value, the stronger the evidence against the null hypothesis.

The standard error in the denominator does the real work. It measures how much the sample statistic — a mean, a proportion, a difference between two means — would typically vary from one random sample to the next. Dividing by it converts a raw difference, in whatever units the data were measured (milliliters, test scores, response times), into a universal scale you can compare against a critical value. [NIST's e-Handbook of Statistical Methods on testing a single population mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc22.htm) builds the one-sample case around exactly this ratio: the test statistic is the sample mean minus the hypothesized mean, divided by the standard error of the mean.

Two samples with identical raw differences can produce very different test statistics once that standard error is accounted for. A 3 mL gap between a sample mean and a claimed value is unremarkable if cans vary by 20 mL from one to the next, but the same 3 mL gap is striking if cans normally vary by only 1 mL — the test statistic captures that distinction automatically, where the raw difference alone cannot.

When the population standard deviation σ is unknown — the normal situation in practice — that standard error is estimated from the sample itself, and the formula becomes the one-sample t-statistic:

```
t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)
```

where x̄ is the sample mean, μ₀ is the hypothesized population mean, s is the sample standard deviation, and n is the sample size. The result follows a t-distribution with `n − 1` degrees of freedom, not a standard normal distribution, because s is itself estimated with error. When σ is known from an outside, trusted source, the same logic produces a z-statistic instead: `z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n)`, compared against the <Link to={routes.article('z-score')}>z-score</Link> distribution rather than the t.

## What Is a Degree of Freedom in Statistics?

A degree of freedom (abbreviated **df**, occasionally written **d.f.**) is one independent piece of information available for estimating something from your data. In a sample of n values, all n observations are free to vary. The moment you use that sample to estimate a parameter — a mean, say — one piece of information is "used up," leaving one fewer degree of freedom for whatever you estimate next.

For a one-sample t-test, you estimate the population mean with the sample mean before computing the sample standard deviation, so:

```
df = n − 1
```

A sample of 16 observations has df = 15. That number tells the t-distribution how heavy its tails should be: **smaller df means heavier tails and larger critical values.** As df grows toward infinity, the t-distribution converges on the standard normal, and its critical values converge on the familiar z-scores — 1.645 at 90%, 1.960 at 95%, 2.576 at 99% confidence.

The same n − 1 shows up in the sample standard deviation formula itself, for the same reason. Dividing the sum of squared deviations by n − 1 instead of n (a correction sometimes called Bessel's correction) compensates for the fact that the sample mean, by construction, is the value that minimizes those squared deviations — using n would systematically underestimate the population variance. [NIST's table of critical values for the Student's t-distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3672.htm) labels its last row "Normal Values," reflecting that convergence. In practice, by roughly 100 degrees of freedom the two distributions are already almost indistinguishable.

Knowing how to find degrees of freedom for other common tests follows the same "data points minus estimated parameters" logic:

[NIST's page on comparing two process means](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section3/prc31.htm) and its [companion page on paired observations](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section3/prc311.htm) confirm the n₁ + n₂ − 2 and "pairs − 1" rules respectively. For the chi-square goodness-of-fit test, [NIST's handbook page](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35f.htm) gives df = k − c, where c is the number of estimated parameters plus one — that reduces to the familiar k − 1 only in the common case where no distribution parameter is estimated from the sample, just the category counts themselves. The pattern never changes: start with the number of independent observations, then subtract one for every parameter you had to estimate before you could compute the statistic. The <Link to={routes.article('chi-square-distribution')}>chi-square distribution guide</Link> covers how that df value shapes the chi-square curve itself.

A paired t-test is worth a second look because it is easy to miscount. With 20 subjects measured before and after a treatment, it is tempting to write df = 20 + 20 − 2 = 38, as if there were two independent samples. But the paired design collapses each before/after pair into a single difference score, leaving 20 differences rather than 40 independent values — so df = 20 − 1 = 19, not 38. Treating paired data as if it were two independent groups is one of the more common degrees-of-freedom mistakes in practice.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Before computing df for a before/after or matched-pairs design, count your rows of data, not your rows of measurements. Twenty subjects measured twice is still 20 independent units — 20 difference scores — so df = n − 1 = 19, never n₁ + n₂ − 2.
</Callout>

## How Do You Read a t Distribution Table?

A t distribution table (also called a table for t distribution, or simply a t table distribution) organizes critical values by two axes: degrees of freedom down the rows, and significance level plus tail count across the columns. Once you have your test statistic and its df, you find the cell where your row and column meet — that cell is the critical value.

    ['20', '1.725', '1.725', '2.086', '2.845'],

    ['∞', '1.645', '1.645', '1.960', '2.576'],
  ]}
/>

A two-tailed test at α = 0.05 splits that 5% evenly between both tails (2.5% each). A one-tailed test at α = 0.05 places the entire 5% in one tail, which is why the one-tailed column always holds a smaller critical value than the two-tailed column at the same α — a smaller rejection threshold is easier to clear, but only in the direction you committed to before collecting data. Notice that the bottom row (∞ df) reproduces the standard normal z-scores exactly, confirming the convergence described above.

Reading down any single column tells its own story: as df falls from 30 to 5, the α = 0.05 two-tailed critical value climbs from 2.042 to 2.571. That increase is the table's way of penalizing small samples — with fewer data points backing up the estimated standard deviation, the test demands a larger test statistic before it will call a result significant.

Printed t distribution tables rarely list every df value — they jump from 20 to 24 to 30, for instance. When your df falls between two listed rows, use the calculator above instead of interpolating by eye; it returns the exact critical value for any whole-number df.
</Callout>

## How Do You Find a Critical Value for a Hypothesis Test?

Finding a critical value takes three inputs, a lookup, and a comparison, in this order:

1. **Choose the significance level (α).** 0.05 (5%) is the conventional default, meaning you accept a 5% chance of rejecting a true null hypothesis (a Type I error). Some fields use the stricter 0.01 or the more lenient 0.10.
2. **Decide one-tailed or two-tailed.** Use two-tailed when the alternative hypothesis is H₁: parameter ≠ value — a difference in either direction counts. Use one-tailed only when the alternative specifies a direction (H₁: parameter > value or < value), decided before looking at the data.
3. **Determine degrees of freedom.** For a one-sample t-test, df = n − 1; see the table above for other designs.
4. **Look up the table or run the calculator.** Find the row matching your df, then the column matching your α and tail count. That cell — or the calculator's output — is the critical value.
5. **Compare and decide.** Reject H₀ if |test statistic| ≥ critical value (two-tailed), or if the signed statistic clears the critical value in the predicted direction (one-tailed). Otherwise, fail to reject.

[NIST's guidance on confidence limits for the mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm) builds a confidence interval from the same critical value this decision rule uses, just applied to an interval estimate instead of a yes/no test — the <Link to={routes.article('null-hypothesis')}>null hypothesis guide</Link> covers how to state H₀ and H₁ correctly before you get this far.

## One-Tailed vs Two-Tailed Tests: Which Critical Value Applies?

The direction you choose changes which critical value you need, and therefore how hard the null hypothesis is to reject:

- **Two-tailed (H₁: μ ≠ μ₀).** The rejection region sits in both tails. At α = 0.05 and df = 15, the critical value is 2.131; reject H₀ if |t| ≥ 2.131.
- **One-tailed (H₁: μ > μ₀ or μ < μ₀).** The rejection region sits in one tail only. At α = 0.05 and df = 15, the critical value is 1.753; reject H₀ if t ≥ 1.753 (upper-tailed) or t ≤ −1.753 (lower-tailed).

The one-tailed critical value (1.753) is smaller than the two-tailed one (2.131), so it is easier to clear — but only in the predicted direction. If the real effect runs the opposite way, a one-tailed test gives you no way to detect it, no matter how extreme the data are. Decide the tail count from your research question before collecting data; switching to one-tailed after seeing a nearly significant two-tailed result is a form of p-hacking that inflates your real Type I error rate above the stated α.

A one-tailed test fits a question like "does the new process produce cans with *more* than 355 mL," where an outcome below 355 mL would never lead to rejecting H₀ regardless of how far below it falls. A two-tailed test fits "does the new process produce cans with a *different* average than 355 mL," where deviation in either direction is relevant. Most quality-control and regulatory settings default to two-tailed tests specifically because an unexpected result in the "wrong" direction is still worth catching.

## How Does the Test Statistic Formula Change Across Different Tests?

The ratio stays the same — observed minus expected, divided by standard error — but the specific formula for a test statistic depends on what you are comparing.

<DataTable
  caption="The test statistic formula by hypothesis test"
  headers={['Test', 'Test statistic formula', 'Distribution used']}
  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[
    ['One-sample t-test', 't = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)', 't, df = n − 1'],
    ['One-sample z-test', 'z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n)', 'Standard normal (z)'],
    ['Two-sample t-test (pooled)', 't = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / (sₚ √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂))', 't, df = n₁ + n₂ − 2'],
    ['Chi-square goodness-of-fit', 'χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E', 'Chi-square, df = k − 1 (no estimated parameters)'],
    ['One-way ANOVA', 'F = (variance between groups) / (variance within groups)', 'F, df₁ = k − 1, df₂ = N − k'],
  ]}
/>

Despite the different symbols, every row answers the same question: how many standard errors away from the null hypothesis does this sample sit? The <Link to={routes.article('t-test')}>t-test guide</Link> works through the one- and two-sample t formulas with fully solved numbers, the <Link to={routes.article('chi-square-test')}>chi-square test guide</Link> walks the goodness-of-fit and independence formulas step by step, and <Link to={routes.article('t-test-vs-z-test')}>t-test vs. z-test</Link> covers exactly when to swap s for a known σ.

The two-sample row's pooled standard deviation, sₚ, is itself a formula worth seeing in full. [NIST's page on comparing two process means](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section3/prc31.htm) builds it from each group's own sample variance, weighted by its degrees of freedom:

```
sₚ = √( ((n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂²) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2) )
```

where s₁ and s₂ are the two groups' sample standard deviations and n₁ and n₂ are their sample sizes. That pooled figure then replaces the single s in the one-sample formula's standard error term.

The pooled two-sample formula above assumes both groups share a common population standard deviation. When that assumption is shaky — one group's data are noticeably more spread out than the other's — Welch's t-test replaces the pooled standard error with one computed from each group's own variance separately, and its degrees of freedom are no longer a clean integer formula but an approximation based on both sample sizes and variances. Software defaults vary, so check before assuming: [R's `t.test()` defaults to Welch's version](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/t.test.html) (`var.equal = FALSE`), while [Python's `scipy.stats.ttest_ind()`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_ind.html) defaults to the pooled, equal-variance version (`equal_var=True`) and needs `equal_var=False` set explicitly to switch to Welch's.

## A Worked Example: Using the Test Statistic Formula Step by Step

**Scenario:** A beverage company claims its cans hold 355 mL on average. A quality inspector samples 16 cans and measures a mean of 352 mL with a sample standard deviation of 6 mL. At α = 0.05, is there statistically significant evidence the true mean differs from 355 mL?

<Figure number={1} caption="The five-step path from stated hypotheses to a reject/fail-to-reject decision for the 16-can sample.">
  <ProcessFlow
    title="Test statistic decision process"
    desc="Five steps: state the hypotheses, compute the test statistic, find degrees of freedom, find the critical value, then compare and decide."
    steps={[
      { label: 'State the hypotheses', detail: 'H0: mu = 355 mL. H1: mu is not 355 mL.' },
      { label: 'Compute the test statistic', detail: 't = (352 - 355) / (6 / sqrt(16)) = -2.00.' },
      { label: 'Find degrees of freedom', detail: 'df = 16 - 1 = 15.' },
      { label: 'Find the critical value', detail: 'Two-tailed, alpha = 0.05, df = 15: t* = 2.131.' },
      { label: 'Compare and decide', detail: '|-2.00| < 2.131, so fail to reject H0.' },
    ]}
    direction="horizontal"
  />
</Figure>

1. **State the hypotheses.** H₀: μ = 355 mL. H₁: μ ≠ 355 mL (two-tailed).
2. **Compute the test statistic.**
   ```
   t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)
     = (352 − 355) / (6 / √16)
     = −3 / (6 / 4)
     = −3 / 1.5
     = −2.00
   ```
3. **Find degrees of freedom.** df = n − 1 = 16 − 1 = 15.
4. **Find the critical value.** At df = 15, two-tailed α = 0.05, the t distribution table gives t* = 2.131.
5. **Compare and decide.** |t| = 2.00 is less than t* = 2.131, so the inspector **fails to reject** the null hypothesis — the sample does not provide strong enough evidence that the cans differ from 355 mL.

If the sample mean had instead been 350.8 mL, the same formula gives t = (350.8 − 355) / 1.5 = −2.80, which exceeds 2.131 in absolute value — that result would reject H₀.

## Finding Test Statistics and Critical Values in R, Python, and Excel

Each major tool replaces the printed t distribution table with a direct function call:

```
R:      qt(0.975, df = 15)        -> 2.131450   # two-tailed critical value, alpha = 0.05
        pt(-2.00, df = 15)        -> 0.03197    # one-tailed p-value for t = -2.00
Python: from scipy.stats import t
        t.ppf(0.975, df=15)       -> 2.131450
        t.cdf(-2.00, df=15)       -> 0.03197
Excel:  =T.INV(0.975, 15)         -> 2.131450
```

R's [`qt()` function](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/TDist.html) returns the quantile (critical value) for a given cumulative probability and df, and its companion `pt()` returns the cumulative probability for a given t and df — the two directions of a t table lookup in one line each. Python's [`scipy.stats.t` object](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.t.html) exposes the same pair through `.ppf()` and `.cdf()`. Excel's [T.INV function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/t-inv-function-2908272b-4e61-4942-9df9-a25fec9b0e2e) returns the left-tailed inverse of the t-distribution — passing it `1 − α/2` and your df gives the two-tailed critical value directly, matching the table and the calculator above.

None of these three require you to compute the test statistic by hand first, either. R's [`t.test()` function](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/t.test.html) and Python's [`scipy.stats.ttest_1samp()`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_1samp.html) each take the raw sample plus the hypothesized mean and return the test statistic, degrees of freedom, and p-value together:

```
R:      t.test(can_volumes, mu = 355)
        # t = -2.00, df = 15, p-value = 0.0639

Python: from scipy.stats import ttest_1samp
        ttest_1samp(can_volumes, popmean=355)
        # TtestResult(statistic=-2.00, pvalue=0.0639, df=15)
```

Excel's [T.TEST function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/t-test-function-d4e08ec3-c545-485f-962e-276f7cbed055) takes two ranges of raw data rather than a single sample against a hypothesized mean, so it is built for comparing two groups (paired or independent) rather than the one-sample case above — for a one-sample test in Excel, compute the test statistic with the formula directly, then pass it to T.DIST.2T or look it up against T.INV as shown.

## Common Mistakes When Working With Test Statistics

- **Using the wrong denominator.** The formula uses `s / √n`, the standard error of the mean, not `s` alone. Dividing by `s` makes the denominator too large and the test statistic too small, under-rejecting false null hypotheses. Fix it by writing out the standard error as its own line before dividing, rather than dividing in a single mental step.
- **Ignoring the sign in a two-tailed test.** Compare the **absolute value** of the test statistic against the critical value — a test statistic of −2.5 is exactly as extreme as +2.5. Fix it by taking the absolute value as a deliberate step before the comparison, not by eyeballing which number looks bigger.
- **Using z when σ is unknown, regardless of sample size.** The t-distribution with df = n − 1 is the correct choice any time the population standard deviation is estimated from the sample rather than known outright — that includes large samples, not just small ones. The z-distribution's lighter tails only become a negligible difference as n grows; they do not become "correct" at n = 30. Fix it by checking whether σ is truly known from an external source, not just assumed, before reaching for a z-table.
- **Misreading the t distribution table columns.** A two-tailed test at α = 0.05 uses the same column as a one-tailed test at α = 0.025. Confirm which column you are in before recording the critical value, since the two labeling conventions sit side by side in most printed tables.
- **Miscounting degrees of freedom on a paired or two-sample design.** Treating paired before/after data as two independent groups inflates df and understates how uncertain the estimate really is. Fix it by asking whether each row of data represents one subject or two before choosing a df formula.
- **Treating "fail to reject" as "proven true."** A test statistic that falls short of the critical value means the data are *consistent with* the null hypothesis, not that the null hypothesis is confirmed. An underpowered sample size is a common reason for a non-significant result even when a real effect exists, so fix it by reporting the result as "no significant difference detected," not "no difference exists."

## Statohub's Take on the Test Statistic

Statohub's position is that the test statistic formula is worth memorizing in its general form — (observed − expected) / standard error — rather than as a list of unrelated symbols for each test. Once that ratio clicks, the t-statistic, z-statistic, and chi-square statistic stop looking like separate topics and start looking like the same idea applied to different data. Degrees of freedom deserve the same treatment: learn the "data points minus estimated parameters" rule once, and every test's df formula follows from it rather than needing separate memorization. A printed t distribution table is worth working through by hand once, for the same reason a times table is worth memorizing once — it builds an intuition for how critical values tighten as sample size grows that an instant calculator answer does not teach on its own.
