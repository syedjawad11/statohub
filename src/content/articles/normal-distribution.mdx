A **normal distribution** is a continuous probability distribution whose bell curve shape is symmetric around a central mean, with values becoming steadily less likely the farther they sit from that center. Statisticians also call it the **Gaussian distribution**, after Carl Friedrich Gauss, and the two names describe exactly the same curve. Every normal distribution bell curve is fixed by just two numbers — the mean and the standard deviation — and the same 68-95-99.7 rule applies no matter what those two numbers are. This guide covers the formula, the standard Gaussian distribution, how to read a normal distribution chart, and a fully worked probability calculation.

## What Is a Normal Distribution?

A normal distribution is a continuous probability distribution that is symmetric about its mean: the left and right halves of the curve are mirror images, and the mean, median, and mode all sit at the same central point. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's entry on the normal distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3661.htm) lists a skewness of exactly 0 and a kurtosis of exactly 3 for the distribution, which is the formal way of saying the curve is perfectly symmetric with no systematic lean toward either tail.

The distribution is also called the Gaussian distribution, after the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss. [The MacTutor History of Mathematics archive at the University of St Andrews](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Gauss/) documents Gauss's 1809 two-volume treatise, *Theoria motus corporum coelestium*, on estimating and refining the orbits of celestial bodies. "Normal distribution" and "Gaussian distribution" refer to exactly the same curve and are used interchangeably in statistics.

A random variable follows a normal distribution when three conditions hold together: the curve is symmetric about the mean, the mean/median/mode coincide at the center, and the tails approach but never touch the horizontal axis. No real dataset is perfectly normal, but many — heights, exam scores, measurement errors, blood pressure readings — are close enough for the normal distribution to be a useful model, largely because the central limit theorem pulls the distribution of sample means toward normal even when the underlying data are not. <Link to={routes.article('central-limit-theorem')}>The central limit theorem article</Link> covers that convergence in depth.

## What Shapes the Bell Curve: Mean and Standard Deviation?

The bell curve normal distribution shape is governed entirely by two parameters, and nothing else about the curve can change independently of them.

**The mean (μ) sets the location.** It is the single peak of the curve and the point where the left and right halves split evenly — exactly 50% of the area sits on each side. Shift μ, and the whole curve slides along the number line without changing shape.

**The standard deviation (σ) sets the spread.** A small σ produces a tall, narrow bell with short tails: values cluster tightly near the mean. A large σ produces a wide, flat bell with long tails: values spread out further. Two normal distribution curves can share the same mean and still look completely different if their standard deviations differ — this is why two exam score distributions with an identical average can have very different numbers of students at the extremes.

For a quick visual check, the inflection points — where the curve switches from curving downward to curving upward — always sit at exactly μ − σ and μ + σ. That is a fast way to read σ directly off a plotted normal distribution chart without doing any arithmetic.

## What Is the Normal Distribution Formula?

The shape of every normal distribution curve is governed by its probability density function (PDF):

```
f(x) = (1 / (σ · √(2π))) · exp( −(x − μ)² / (2σ²) )
```

Where `μ` is the mean, `σ` is the standard deviation, `π ≈ 3.14159`, and `exp(·)` is the exponential function. The [NIST/SEMATECH handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3661.htm) gives this exact formula and notes that the special case μ = 0, σ = 1 is called the standard normal distribution — the subject of the next section.

The exponent `−(x − μ)² / (2σ²)` is zero only when `x = μ`, which is where the PDF reaches its peak. As `x` moves away from `μ`, the exponent grows more negative and the PDF value shrinks toward zero, producing the bell taper. Crucially, `f(x)` is a *density*, not a probability — for the standard normal distribution, `f(0) ≈ 0.399`, a number that can exceed 1 for a narrow enough σ and is never itself a probability. The probability that X falls in a range `[a, b]` is the area under the curve between those two points, found with the cumulative distribution function (CDF) rather than the PDF directly — in practice, that means a z-table, software, or the calculator below, not hand integration.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Density is not probability, and that has a sharp consequence: for a continuous distribution like the normal, the probability of landing on any single exact value is 0. Only a range — `P(a < X < b)` — has a nonzero probability, which is why every calculation in this guide works with an interval or a one-sided bound, never a lone point.
</Callout>

## What Is the Standard Gaussian Distribution, and How Do You Convert to Z-Scores?

The standard Gaussian distribution — also called the standard normal distribution — is the specific case where μ = 0 and σ = 1, written `Z ~ N(0, 1)`. It matters because any normal distribution can be converted to it through standardization, which is what makes a single set of tables and software functions work for every possible mean and standard deviation.

Given a value `x` from a normal distribution with mean `μ` and standard deviation `σ`, its z-score measures how many standard deviations above or below the mean it sits:

```
z = (x − μ) / σ
```

For example, with test scores normally distributed at μ = 70 and σ = 10, a score of 85 has:

```
z = (85 − 70) / 10 = 1.5
```

That score sits 1.5 standard deviations above the mean. Looking up `Φ(1.5)` — the cumulative probability at z = 1.5, as tabulated in [NIST/SEMATECH's cumulative distribution function table for the standard normal distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3671.htm) — gives 0.9332, meaning about 93.3% of scores fall below 85. Reading the table itself (rows, columns, and the two common formats) is covered step by step in <Link to={routes.article('z-table')}>the z-table guide</Link>; this section is about the standardization move itself, which is what makes that lookup possible for any mean and standard deviation at all.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
A positive z-score always means the value sits above the mean; a negative z-score always means it sits below. The size of |z| tells you how unusual the value is — |z| > 2 is already in roughly the top or bottom 2.5% of a normal distribution.
</Callout>

## What Does the Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7) Tell You?

The empirical rule gives three probability benchmarks that hold for every normal distribution, regardless of its specific μ and σ.

In a dataset of adult heights with μ = 175 cm and σ = 7 cm, the rule says about 68% of people fall between 168 cm and 182 cm, about 95% between 161 cm and 189 cm, and nearly everyone — 99.7% — between 154 cm and 196 cm. A value more than 2σ from the mean occurs only about 5% of the time under a normal distribution; one beyond 3σ is rare enough (roughly 1 in 370 observations) that many quality-control systems treat it as a signal something changed. The [NIST/SEMATECH handbook's discussion of variables control charts](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc32.htm) documents exactly this use: standard control limits are set at the process mean ± 3 standard deviations, directly borrowing the empirical rule's 99.7% benchmark to flag unusual process behavior.

The rule is specific to the normal distribution — it does not hold for a <Link to={routes.article('skewed-distribution')}>skewed distribution</Link>, where the percentages within one or two standard deviations can be very different. Chebyshev's inequality offers a weaker, distribution-free bound (at least `1 − 1/k²` of any dataset falls within k standard deviations of the mean) that holds everywhere but gives much looser estimates than 68-95-99.7 does for data that actually is normal.

## How Do You Read a Normal Distribution Chart?

A normal distribution chart plots probability density on the vertical axis against the value on the horizontal axis, producing the bell curve. The single rule that makes the chart readable: **area corresponds to probability, not height.**

The total area under any normal distribution curve equals exactly 1. The tallest point is always at the mean, which splits the area exactly 50/50. The probability that X falls between two values `a` and `b` is the shaded area between them, computed from the standard normal CDF, `Φ(z)`:

```
P(a < X < b) = Φ(z_b) − Φ(z_a)
```

where `z_a = (a − μ) / σ` and `z_b = (b − μ) / σ`. [OpenStax's Introductory Statistics, chapter 6](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/6-1-the-standard-normal-distribution) works through this same area-based logic for the standard normal distribution before extending it to any μ and σ. A useful rule of thumb when scanning normal distribution curves visually: shading one bell-width on either side of the peak captures roughly two-thirds of the area, and shading two bell-widths captures nearly all of it — the empirical rule, read directly off the shape of the chart.

## Where Does the Normal Distribution Show Up in Practice?

The normal distribution appears across science, industry, and statistics itself for a specific reason: many measurements are the sum of many small, independent sources of variation, and sums of independent random effects converge toward a normal shape as the central limit theorem predicts. Adult height, blood pressure, measurement error, and manufacturing tolerances all approximate normal distribution curves for this reason.

Inferential statistics leans on the same fact from the other direction. The t-test, z-test, ANOVA, and linear regression all assume that residuals are approximately normal, and confidence intervals for a population mean rely on the sampling distribution of the mean being approximately normal for large samples — <Link to={routes.article('normality-tests')}>the normality-tests guide</Link> covers how to formally check that assumption with Shapiro-Wilk, Anderson-Darling, and related tests rather than eyeballing a histogram. When the assumption holds, these methods produce accurate p-values; when it fails badly — strong skew, heavy tails, multiple modes — a transformation or a <Link to={routes.article('nonparametric-tests')}>nonparametric alternative</Link> is usually the safer path.

## A Worked Example: Probability a Test Score Falls Between 60 and 90

**Setup.** A standardized test is normally distributed with mean μ = 70 and standard deviation σ = 10. Find the probability that a randomly selected student scores between 60 and 90.

<Figure number={1} caption="Four steps from the raw bounds to the interval probability for the test-score example.">
  <ProcessFlow
    title="Normal distribution probability process"
    desc="Four connected steps: identify the bounds 60 and 90; convert each to a z-score; look up the cumulative probabilities; subtract to get the interval probability of 0.8185."
    steps={[
      { label: 'Identify the bounds', detail: 'a = 60, b = 90.' },
      { label: 'Convert to z-scores', detail: 'z_a = (60−70)/10 = −1.00; z_b = (90−70)/10 = 2.00.' },
      { label: 'Look up Φ(z)', detail: 'Φ(−1.00) = 0.1587; Φ(2.00) = 0.9772.' },
      { label: 'Subtract', detail: 'P(60<X<90) = 0.9772 − 0.1587 = 0.8185.' },
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

1. **Identify the bounds.** Lower bound `a = 60`; upper bound `b = 90`.

2. **Convert to z-scores.**

```
z_a = (60 − 70) / 10 = −10 / 10 = −1.00
z_b = (90 − 70) / 10 =  20 / 10 =  2.00
```

3. **Look up the cumulative probabilities** from a standard normal table (or the calculator below):

```
Φ(−1.00) = 0.1587   (15.87% of scores fall below 60)
Φ( 2.00) = 0.9772   (97.72% of scores fall below 90)
```

4. **Subtract to get the interval probability.**

```
P(60 < X < 90) = Φ(2.00) − Φ(−1.00) = 0.9772 − 0.1587 = 0.8185
```

About **81.85%** of students score between 60 and 90.

**Verification using the empirical rule.** The interval [60, 90] runs from −1σ to +2σ around the mean. Because the normal distribution is symmetric, half of the ±1σ band (half of 68%, or 34%) plus half of the ±2σ band (half of 95%, or 47.5%) covers exactly the −1σ-to-+2σ span: 34% + 47.5% = 81.5%, close to the exact 81.85% computed above. **Decision:** 81.85% of students fall in this range, so a score of 60 or 90 is not unusual on its own — only scores well outside roughly 50 to 90 (beyond ±2σ) would be worth flagging as atypical.

## How Do You Calculate Normal Distribution Probabilities in Excel, Python, or R?

Software removes the table-lookup step once you have the right mean, standard deviation, and bound.

```
Excel:   =NORM.DIST(x, mean, standard_dev, TRUE)     returns P(X ≤ x)
         =NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE)                       returns Φ(z) for a standardized z

Python:  from scipy.stats import norm
         norm.cdf(x, loc=mean, scale=sd)             # P(X ≤ x)
         norm.cdf(z)                                  # Φ(z) for the standard normal

R:       pnorm(x, mean = mu, sd = sigma)              # P(X ≤ x)
         pnorm(z)                                      # Φ(z) for the standard normal
```

[Microsoft's documentation for `NORM.DIST`](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/norm-dist-function-edb1cc14-a21c-4e53-839d-8082074c9f8d) and [`NORM.S.DIST`](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/norm-s-dist-function-1e787282-3832-4520-a9ae-bd2a8d99ba88) covers the Excel syntax above, including the cumulative-vs-density flag. [SciPy's `scipy.stats.norm` reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.norm.html) documents `cdf`, along with `pdf`, `ppf` (the inverse CDF), and the location/scale parameterization Python uses instead of separate mean and standard-deviation arguments. [R's documentation for the normal distribution functions](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/Normal.html) covers `dnorm`, `pnorm`, and `qnorm`, which play the same density, CDF, and inverse-CDF roles. For an interval probability in any of the three, compute the CDF at both bounds and subtract — the same `Φ(z_b) − Φ(z_a)` logic from the worked example above, just without the manual table lookup.

## Common Mistakes with the Normal Distribution

- **Assuming all data is normal.** Income, survival times, and stock returns are typically skewed or heavy-tailed. Inspect a histogram or a normal probability plot before applying normal-distribution methods, and run a formal check with <Link to={routes.article('normality-tests')}>a normality test</Link> rather than relying on a visual guess alone.
- **Confusing the PDF with a probability.** `f(x)` is a density and can exceed 1; it is never itself a probability. Probability comes only from the area under the curve between two points.
- **Misreading σ as the variance.** The formula and the z-score both use σ (standard deviation), not σ² (variance). If you only have the variance, take its square root first.
- **Applying the empirical rule to non-normal data.** The 68-95-99.7 benchmarks are specific to the normal distribution; for a skewed distribution, the actual percentages within one or two standard deviations may differ substantially.
- **Forgetting the continuity correction.** When using the normal distribution to approximate a discrete distribution such as the <Link to={routes.article('binomial-distribution')}>binomial distribution</Link>, a ±0.5 continuity correction improves accuracy — for example, approximate P(X = 10) with P(9.5 < X < 10.5), not a direct density lookup.

## Statohub's Take on the Normal Distribution

Statohub's position is that the normal distribution earns its central place in statistics honestly — it is the limiting shape the central limit theorem predicts for sums of independent effects, not a default assumption to reach for out of habit. The practical discipline is to treat "approximately normal" as a claim to check, with a plot and a formal test, rather than something to assume because the math downstream gets easier if it is true.
