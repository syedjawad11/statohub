The **interquartile range** (IQR) is the difference between the third quartile (Q3) and the first quartile (Q1) of a sorted data set, and it measures how spread out the middle 50 percent of your values are. What is the interquartile range useful for that other spread measures aren't? It ignores the extreme high and low values entirely, which makes it the spread statistic of choice whenever a data set might contain outliers or a skewed tail. This guide covers the IQR formula, shows you how to find the interquartile range by hand with a worked example, and pinpoints exactly where it beats — and where it loses to — the range and the standard deviation.

## What Is the Interquartile Range?

The interquartile range is a measure of statistical dispersion built from two specific values in a sorted data set: the first quartile (Q1) and the third quartile (Q3). Quartiles divide sorted data into four equal quarters, and the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda356.htm) defines the interquartile range plainly as "the value of the 75th percentile minus the value of the 25th percentile," adding that this measure of scale "attempts to measure the variability of points near the center."

- **Q1 (first quartile):** the value below which 25 percent of the data falls.
- **Q2 (second quartile):** the median — half the values lie below it.
- **Q3 (third quartile):** the value below which 75 percent of the data falls.

```
IQR = Q3 − Q1
```

Because Q1 marks the bottom of the middle half and Q3 marks the top, the IQR spans the central 50 percent of the data and says nothing at all about the outer quarters on either end. Quartiles are a specific case of percentiles — Q1 is the 25th percentile and Q3 is the 75th — so the <Link to={routes.article('percentiles')}>percentiles guide</Link> is the place to go for how percentile ranks work more generally; this page focuses on what happens once you land on exactly those two.

## What Is the IQR Formula?

So what is interquartile range, arithmetically speaking? One subtraction:

```
IQR = Q3 − Q1
```

The result carries the same units as the original data: an IQR of 8 minutes means the middle half of your measurements spans 8 minutes. The arithmetic is trivial — the real work is locating Q1 and Q3 correctly, and that's where methods diverge. [R's `quantile()` documentation](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/quantile.html) lists nine distinct sample-quantile algorithms (selected with its `type` argument, defaulting to type 7), and [NumPy's `percentile()` function](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.percentile.html) offers a matching set of interpolation methods, with `linear` as the default. The step-by-step median-split method below is the version most commonly taught in introductory statistics courses, and it's what this guide uses throughout; expect small differences if you check a result against a spreadsheet or statistical package, especially on a small sample.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
If a classmate, a colleague, or a piece of software gets a slightly different Q1 or Q3 than you did by hand, check which quartile algorithm it used before assuming either one is wrong — the Excel/R/Python comparison further down this page shows two common methods landing 3.5 apart on the very same nine numbers. That gap shrinks as the sample grows, but there's no fixed sample size where it disappears entirely, so it's still worth checking on a larger data set too.
</Callout>

## How Do You Find the Interquartile Range?

Here's how to find interquartile range by hand, in five steps: sort the data, find the median, split the data at the median, find the median of each half, then subtract.

### Step 1: Sort the data from smallest to largest

Arrange every value in ascending order. Skipping this step guarantees a wrong Q1 and Q3.

### Step 2: Find the median (Q2)

Count the values. If the count is **odd**, Q2 is the single middle value. If it is **even**, Q2 is the average of the two middle values.

### Step 3: Split the data at the median

Divide the sorted values into a lower half and an upper half. When the total count is **odd**, exclude the median itself from both halves. When the count is **even**, the two middle values each belong to their own half, with no exclusion needed.

### Step 4: Find Q1 and Q3

Q1 is the median of the lower half; Q3 is the median of the upper half. Apply the same rule from Step 2 to each half.

### Step 5: Subtract Q1 from Q3

```
IQR = Q3 − Q1
```

## A Worked Example: Spread in Weekly Sales Calls

A sales manager wants to know how consistent call volume was across the team last week, before deciding whether to flag the spread as a problem. Nine reps logged these call counts: 14, 19, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 42 (already sorted, n = 9).

```
Step 1 — Data is already sorted: 14, 19, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 42
Step 2 — Median (Q2): position 5 of 9 → Q2 = 27
Step 3 — Lower half (exclude 27): 14, 19, 22, 25
          Upper half (exclude 27): 29, 31, 35, 42
Step 4 — Q1 = median(14, 19, 22, 25) = (19 + 22) / 2 = 20.5
          Q3 = median(29, 31, 35, 42) = (31 + 35) / 2 = 33
Step 5 — IQR = 33 − 20.5 = 12.5
```

The interquartile range is **12.5 calls**. The middle half of the reps' weekly call counts spans 12.5 calls, while the overall range is 42 − 14 = 28 — more than twice as wide, because the range also counts the two most extreme reps that the IQR deliberately excludes.

**Decision.** The manager should report the median (27 calls) and the IQR (12.5 calls) as the headline spread figures for this team, not the range — a 28-call range sounds like a wide spread, but the IQR shows the middle 50 percent of reps cluster within 12.5 calls of each other, which is a much more defensible read of "typical" performance. The next section shows exactly why that choice holds up even if one rep's count turns out to be unusual.

## Why Does the IQR Resist Outliers?

The IQR is a **resistant statistic**: extreme values at either end of a sorted data set cannot move it, because Q1 and Q3 are computed from the inner portion of the data, not the extremes. Replace the largest value in the worked example — 42 — with an outlier of 95:

```
Modified data: 14, 19, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 95
Median: still 27 (position 5 of 9, unchanged)
Lower half: 14, 19, 22, 25 → Q1 = 20.5 (unchanged)
Upper half: 29, 31, 35, 95 → Q3 = median(29, 31, 35, 95) = 33 (unchanged)
IQR = 33 − 20.5 = 12.5  (identical to the original)
```

The range, by contrast, leaps from 28 to 81 (95 − 14) — nearly triple. The IQR holds steady at 12.5 because 95 sits outside the middle 50 percent entirely, so it plays no role in determining either quartile.

This resistance is exactly why the IQR anchors the **box** in a box-and-whisker plot. [NIST/SEMATECH's box plot reference](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/boxplot.htm) describes the construction directly: plot the median, then "draw a box between the lower and upper quartiles" — so the box's width is the IQR by definition — and it is why the IQR also powers the standard rule for flagging outliers in the first place. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's guidance on outlier detection](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc16.htm) builds exactly this kind of fence from the IQR: a lower inner fence at Q1 − 1.5×IQR and an upper inner fence at Q3 + 1.5×IQR, with points beyond an outer fence at 3×IQR flagged as extreme outliers. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, Section 2.3](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-3-measures-of-the-location-of-the-data) sets the same rule in plainer terms: "A value is suspected to be a potential outlier if it is less than (1.5)(IQR) below the first quartile or more than (1.5)(IQR) above the third quartile." For the full mechanics of applying that rule — including how to compute the fences and decide what to do with a flagged point — see the <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-outliers')}>outlier detection guide</Link>; for how the box and whiskers are drawn from these same numbers, see the <Link to={routes.article('histogram-vs-boxplot')}>histogram vs. boxplot guide</Link>.

## When Should You Use the IQR Instead of the Range or Standard Deviation?

Reach for the IQR whenever a data set might contain outliers or a skewed tail and you want a spread measure that describes what's typical rather than what's extreme. Reach for the range only for a fast, disposable first pass, and reach for the standard deviation when you need a measure built from every value and your data don't have extreme outliers distorting it.

## How Do You Calculate the IQR in Excel, R, or Python?

All three tools can return a different Q1 and Q3 than the median-split method above, because each one defaults to a different quartile algorithm. Running the nine call counts (14, 19, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 42) through each tool's default makes the gap concrete.

```
Excel:  =QUARTILE.INC(A1:A9, 3) - QUARTILE.INC(A1:A9, 1)
        → Q1 = 22, Q3 = 31, IQR = 9

R:      calls = c(14, 19, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 42)
        IQR(calls)                       # uses quantile(type = 7) by default → 9

Python: import statistics
        calls = [14, 19, 22, 25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 42]
        statistics.quantiles(calls, n=4)                  # exclusive (default) → [20.5, 27, 33], IQR = 12.5
        statistics.quantiles(calls, n=4, method='inclusive')  # → [22, 27, 31], IQR = 9
```

Excel's [`QUARTILE.INC` function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/quartile-inc-function-1bbacc80-5075-42f1-aed6-47d735c4819d), [NumPy's `percentile()`](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.percentile.html) with its default `linear` method, and [R's `IQR()` function](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/IQR.html), which computes quartiles via `quantile()` with `type = 7` by default, all agree with each other on this data set: Q1 = 22, Q3 = 31, IQR = **9** — not the 12.5 the median-split method produced above. R's own documentation is explicit about the mismatch, noting that `IQR()` "computes the quartiles using the `quantile` function rather than following Tukey's recommendations." Python breaks the pattern: [the standard library's `statistics.quantiles()`](https://docs.python.org/3/library/statistics.html#statistics.quantiles) defaults to `method='exclusive'`, which reproduces the median-split answer of 12.5 exactly — switch it to `method='inclusive'` and it matches Excel and R instead. [SciPy's `scipy.stats.iqr()`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.iqr.html) exposes the same `interpolation` parameter if you need to match a specific convention there too.

<Callout variant="note" title="Neither answer is wrong">
A hand-calculated IQR of 12.5 and a spreadsheet's IQR of 9 on the same nine numbers are both correct for the method each one uses. The gap matters most on small samples; state which method you used whenever the exact Q1/Q3 values matter, not just the final IQR.
</Callout>

## What Mistakes Do People Make When Calculating the IQR?

- **Forgetting to sort the data.** Quartile calculations require ascending order; applying the steps to unsorted data produces a Q1 and Q3 that mean nothing.
- **Including the median in both halves.** When n is odd, the median is a single data point and must be excluded from both the lower and upper halves before finding Q1 and Q3. Including it inflates one quartile and deflates the other.
- **Reversing Q1 and Q3.** Q1 is always the smaller quartile and Q3 the larger one, so IQR = Q3 − Q1 is always zero or positive. A negative result means the two were swapped.
- **Assuming every tool agrees.** As the comparison above shows, the median-split method and the default linear-interpolation method built into Excel, R, and Python can return noticeably different Q1 and Q3 values on the same small data set, even though every calculation is implemented correctly.
- **Treating the IQR as a percentage.** The IQR is an absolute measure in the same units as the data. An IQR of 8 points is a spread of 8 points, not 8 percent.
- **Reporting a single IQR without stating the method.** State which quartile method produced your Q1 and Q3, especially on a small data set where — as the comparison above shows — the choice of method visibly changes the answer, so a reader or grader can reproduce your number exactly.

## Statohub's Take on the Interquartile Range

Statohub's position is that the IQR deserves to be the default spread statistic the moment a data set might contain outliers, a skewed tail, or values you haven't fully audited yet — which, in practice, describes most real-world data. The range is faster and the standard deviation is more familiar, but neither one tells you what "typical" looks like once a handful of extreme values are in the mix. Report the median and IQR together, and reach for the mean and standard deviation only once you've checked that the extremes aren't quietly running the show.
