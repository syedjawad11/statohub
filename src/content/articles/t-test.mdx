The **paired t-test** is a formula that converts two related measurements per subject — a before-and-after score, say — into a single difference score, then tests whether the average of those differences departs from zero by more than chance explains. It is the formula half of a design question; the matching design question is answered in full in Statohub's <Link to={routes.article('paired-vs-independent-t-test')}>paired vs. independent t-test guide</Link>. This article works through the t-test equation itself: the general ratio every version shares, the paired difference t-test formula in full, the two-sample t-test formula (pooled and Welch), and fully computed numeric examples for both.

## What Is the T-Test Equation?

Every t-test equation, whatever the design, reduces to the same structural ratio:

```
t = (observed effect) / (standard error of that effect)
```

The **student t-test formula** gets its name from William Sealy Gosset, who worked out the distribution while analyzing small samples of hops and barley at the Guinness brewery in Dublin and published it under the pen name "Student." He needed a distribution that stayed valid when the sample size was too small for the normal approximation to hold, because replacing the unknown population standard deviation σ with the sample standard deviation s introduces extra uncertainty that a normal curve understates. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics*, §8.2](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/8-2-a-single-population-mean-using-the-student-t-distribution) recounts how Gosset ran into this problem. The practical result is a distribution with heavier tails than the normal curve, so a t-statistic has to clear a higher threshold than the equivalent <Link to={routes.article('z-score')}>z-score</Link> would need before a result counts as significant — the <Link to={routes.article('t-test-vs-z-test')}>t-test vs. z-test guide</Link> covers exactly when each distribution applies.

The table below lines up the t-test formula for each common design side by side. The <Link to={routes.article('chi-square-test')}>chi-square test</Link>, built on the <Link to={routes.article('chi-square-distribution')}>chi-square distribution</Link>, and ANOVA's F-statistic follow the same observed-over-standard-error logic with different symbols, as the <Link to={routes.article('test-statistic')}>test statistic guide</Link> covers in detail.

## How Do You Derive the Paired Difference T-Test Formula From Your Data?

The **paired difference t-test** starts by reducing two columns of related numbers to one column of differences. For each of the n pairs, compute:

```
dᵢ = (measurement 1)ᵢ − (measurement 2)ᵢ
```

using the same subtraction direction for every pair. From the n difference scores, compute their mean and standard deviation, then plug both into the one-sample t formula:

```
d̄ = (Σ dᵢ) / n

s_d = √( Σ(dᵢ − d̄)² / (n − 1) )

t = d̄ / (s_d / √n)
```

[NIST/SEMATECH's e-Handbook, §7.3.1.1 — Analysis of Paired Observations](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section3/prc311.htm) defines paired data precisely this way: two samples are paired when the i-th measurement in the first sample is naturally tied to the i-th measurement in the second, and builds this exact formula from that definition. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics*, §10.4 — Matched or Paired Samples](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/10-4-matched-or-paired-samples) describes the same reduction: once the differences are calculated, the population mean of those differences is tested with a Student's-t test for a single population mean, using n − 1 degrees of freedom. The null hypothesis is H₀: the true mean difference equals zero — the <Link to={routes.article('null-hypothesis')}>null hypothesis guide</Link> covers how to state that formally for a one-tailed or two-tailed alternative. The result follows a t-distribution with **df = n − 1**, where n counts pairs, not the 2n raw measurements you started with.

<Callout variant="quote" source="NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, §7.3.1.1" sourceHref="https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section3/prc311.htm">
The data are said to be paired if the i-th measurement on the first sample is naturally paired with the i-th measurement on the second sample.
</Callout>

Because s_d is computed on the differences rather than on the raw scores, variation that is common to both measurements on the same subject cancels out before it ever reaches the formula — that is the mechanical reason the paired difference t-test formula is usually more powerful than treating the same data as two independent groups.

## What Is the Two-Sample T-Test Formula?

The **two-sample t-test** (also called the 2 sample t-test, or the t test for two samples) compares the means of two separate groups directly, without reducing anything to differences first. [NIST/SEMATECH's e-Handbook, §7.3.1 — Do Two Processes Have the Same Mean?](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section3/prc31.htm) derives the formula in both forms below, depending on whether the two population variances can be assumed equal; [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics*, §10.1](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/10-1-two-population-means-with-unknown-standard-deviations) covers only the unequal-variance (Aspin-Welch) form and explicitly advises against pooling the two sample variances.

**Equal variances (pooled).** First pool the two sample variances, weighted by their degrees of freedom, into a single pooled standard deviation s_p:

```
s_p = √( ((n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂²) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2) )

t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / (s_p × √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂))
```

with df = n₁ + n₂ − 2.

**Unequal variances (Welch's).** Replace the pooled term with each group's own variance divided by its own sample size:

```
t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)
```

The degrees of freedom are no longer a clean integer; they come from the Welch–Satterthwaite approximation, which NIST's handbook gives in full. Welch's version remains valid whether or not the variances are actually equal. Software defaults differ on which formula runs by default: [R's `t.test()` function](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/t.test.html) defaults to Welch's formula (`var.equal = FALSE`), while [Python's `scipy.stats.ttest_ind()`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_ind.html) defaults the other way, to the pooled formula (`equal_var=True`).

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
When you are not sure whether two groups share a population variance, default to the Welch formula rather than the pooled one. It costs you almost nothing when the variances genuinely are equal and the two sample sizes match, and it protects you from a distorted Type I error rate on the pooled test when the variances and the sample sizes are both unequal — the pooled standard error can end up too small or too large depending on which group has the larger variance and which has the larger n.
</Callout>

A quick illustration of the pooled formula: a manufacturing line samples n₁ = 8 units from Process A (mean fill weight x̄₁ = 74 g, s₁ = 6 g) and n₂ = 8 units from Process B (x̄₂ = 68 g, s₂ = 8 g). Their sample standard deviations are close enough to pool:

```
s_p = √( (7 × 36 + 7 × 64) / 14 ) = √(700 / 14) = √50 ≈ 7.071

t = (74 − 68) / (7.071 × √(1/8 + 1/8)) = 6 / (7.071 × 0.5) = 6 / 3.536 ≈ 1.70
```

df = 8 + 8 − 2 = 14. At α = 0.05 two-tailed, [NIST's table of critical values for the Student's t-distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3672.htm) gives a critical value of about 2.145 at df = 14, so |t| = 1.70 falls short and the two processes' means are not shown to differ at the 5% level with this sample size.

## What Do You Need to Check Before Running a T-Test?

All versions of the t-test equation share four conditions worth confirming before you trust the result.

- **Independence.** Observations are independent of each other within a group; in a paired design, the pairs themselves must be independent even though the two readings inside each pair are deliberately correlated.
- **Approximate normality.** The data (or the difference scores, in a paired design) should be roughly normal, or the sample large enough for the central limit theorem to cover minor departures — OpenStax's guidance above notes that distribution shape stops mattering once both samples are reasonably large, and matters most when either sample is small.
- **Interval or ratio measurement.** The outcome must sit on a continuous scale. For ranked or ordinal data, the <Link to={routes.article('mann-whitney-u-test')}>Mann-Whitney U test</Link> or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test are the standard nonparametric substitutes.
- **Equal variances, for the pooled two-sample formula only.** This assumption does not apply to the paired formula or to Welch's two-sample formula, both of which sidestep it entirely.

These four conditions, and what the paired and two-sample designs share beyond the formula itself, are covered in full in the <Link to={routes.article('paired-vs-independent-t-test')}>paired vs. independent t-test guide</Link>'s assumptions section.

## How Do You Run a Paired or Two-Sample T-Test in Software?

Each major tool keeps the paired and two-sample t-test formulas behind separate function calls or arguments, and mixing them up is an easy way to get the wrong standard error.

<DataTable
  caption="Running a paired vs. two-sample t-test in R, Python, and Excel"
  headers={['Tool', 'Paired t-test', 'Two-sample t-test']}
  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[
    ['R', 't.test(before, after, paired = TRUE)', 't.test(group1, group2)  # Welch by default'],
    ['Python (SciPy)', 'scipy.stats.ttest_rel(before, after)', 'scipy.stats.ttest_ind(group1, group2, equal_var=False)'],
    ['Excel', 'T.TEST(array1, array2, tails, 1)', 'T.TEST(array1, array2, tails, 2 or 3)'],
  ]}
/>

In R, [`t.test()`](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/t.test.html) switches entirely from the two-sample Welch formula to the paired difference formula the moment you add `paired = TRUE` — it then needs the two raw vectors to be the same length and in matching order, since it computes the differences internally. In Python, [`scipy.stats.ttest_rel()`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_rel.html) is the paired counterpart to [`scipy.stats.ttest_ind()`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_ind.html); the independent-samples function defaults to the pooled, equal-variance formula (`equal_var=True`) and needs that argument flipped to `False` to switch to Welch's. Excel's [T.TEST function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/t-test-function-d4e08ec3-c545-485f-962e-276f7cbed055) takes a `type` argument that picks the formula directly: 1 for paired, 2 for the pooled two-sample formula, and 3 for Welch's unequal-variance formula — all three return a p-value rather than the t-statistic itself, so pair the function with a manual t calculation if you need the statistic too.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Before running either function, check that your paired data really are ordered consistently — row 3 in your "before" vector has to be the same subject as row 3 in your "after" vector, or `paired = TRUE` / `ttest_rel()` will silently compute the wrong differences.
</Callout>

## A Worked Example: Paired T-Test for a Six-Week Diet Programme

A nutritionist tests whether a six-week diet programme reduces body weight. Six participants are weighed before and after, in pounds. Pairing each participant with themselves removes the large person-to-person differences in starting weight from the analysis.

| Participant | Before (lb) | After (lb) | d = Before − After |
|-------------|-------------|------------|---------------------|
| 1 | 185 | 180 | 5 |
| 2 | 192 | 185 | 7 |
| 3 | 170 | 169 | 1 |
| 4 | 178 | 172 | 6 |
| 5 | 203 | 196 | 7 |
| 6 | 190 | 183 | 7 |

  <ProcessFlow

    desc="Five steps: compute the differences, find the mean and standard deviation of the differences, compute the t statistic, find degrees of freedom, then compare to the critical value and decide."

      { label: 'Find mean and SD of d', detail: 'd-bar = 33 / 6 = 5.5 lb. s_d = sqrt(27.5 / 5) = 2.345 lb.' },
      { label: 'Compute the t statistic', detail: 't = 5.5 / (2.345 / sqrt(6)) = 5.5 / 0.957 = 5.74.' },
      { label: 'Find degrees of freedom', detail: 'df = 6 - 1 = 5.' },
      { label: 'Compare and decide', detail: 'Critical value at df=5, two-tailed 0.05, is 2.571. 5.74 > 2.571, so reject H0.' },
    ]}
    direction="horizontal"
  />
</Figure>

1. **Mean difference.** d̄ = (5 + 7 + 1 + 6 + 7 + 7) / 6 = 33 / 6 = **5.5 lb**.
2. **Standard deviation of the differences.** The squared deviations from 5.5 are 0.25, 2.25, 20.25, 0.25, 2.25, and 2.25, summing to 27.50. So s_d² = 27.50 / (6 − 1) = 5.50, and s_d = √5.50 ≈ **2.345 lb**.
3. **Standard error and t.** SE = s_d / √n = 2.345 / √6 ≈ 2.345 / 2.449 ≈ 0.957 lb. t = d̄ / SE = 5.5 / 0.957 ≈ **5.74**.
4. **Degrees of freedom.** df = n − 1 = 6 − 1 = **5**.
5. **Decision.** At α = 0.05 two-tailed, [NIST's table of critical values for the Student's t-distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3672.htm) gives a critical t-value of about 2.571 at df = 5. Since |t| = 5.74 exceeds 2.571, reject H₀: the programme produced a statistically significant reduction in body weight (mean decrease = 5.5 lb, t(5) = 5.74, p ≈ 0.002).

Individual starting weights in this sample ranged from 170 lb to 203 lb — a 33 lb spread that would swamp the 5.5 lb average reduction if the six before-scores and six after-scores had instead been analyzed as two unrelated groups of six. Pairing each person with their own earlier measurement removed that between-subject noise from the calculation entirely, which is the practical reason the paired difference t-test formula produced such a large t-statistic here.

## Common Mistakes When Applying the T-Test Formula

- **Plugging raw before/after scores into a two-sample formula.** Running the pooled or Welch formula directly on the "before" column and the "after" column, instead of first reducing them to difference scores, discards the within-subject correlation that makes the paired design powerful and tends to produce a far smaller, less significant t-statistic. Fix it by computing dᵢ for every pair before you touch the t formula.
- **Counting raw measurements instead of pairs for degrees of freedom.** Six subjects measured twice gives df = 5 (six pairs minus one), not df = 10. Fix it by asking how many independent difference scores you actually have before applying n − 1.
- **Defaulting to the pooled two-sample formula without checking the variances.** The pooled formula assumes the two groups share a population variance; when the sample standard deviations differ *and* the two group sizes differ, the pooled test's actual Type I error rate drifts away from the stated α (too small or too large, depending on which group has the larger variance and which has the larger n). Fix it by comparing s₁ and s₂ first, and reaching for Welch's formula whenever they look meaningfully different — Welch's formula stays valid regardless of group size.
- **Subtracting in inconsistent directions.** If some differences are computed as Before − After and others as After − Before, positive and negative values cancel out and bias d̄ toward zero. Fix it by picking one subtraction order and verifying every row follows it before summing.
- **Reporting the p-value alone.** A complete result states the t-statistic, the degrees of freedom, and the p-value together — for example, t(5) = 5.74, p ≈ 0.002 — because the t and df let a reader judge the effect size and the test used, not just whether it cleared 0.05.
- **Choosing the wrong tail direction.** A two-tailed test checks whether the effect differs from zero in either direction; a one-tailed test checks only one predicted direction and should only be chosen before the data are collected, based on the research question, not after seeing which direction a nearly-significant result happened to point.

## Statohub's Take on the T-Test Formula

Statohub's position is that the paired and two-sample t-test formulas are worth learning as one idea with two inputs, not two unrelated equations. Both divide an observed effect by a standard error built from exactly the data you have — a column of differences in the paired case, two separate groups in the two-sample case. Once that shared structure is visible, deciding which standard error to build is a data-structure question, answered once per study, rather than a fresh formula to memorize every time.
