Mean median mode range are the four measures you meet in every introductory statistics course, and each one tells you something different about the same list of numbers. The mean is the arithmetic average. The median is the value sitting in the exact middle once the data is sorted. The mode is the value that occurs most often. The range is the gap between the largest and smallest values. Together, these four numbers give you a complete first read of any dataset: where it centers, which value repeats, and how far it spreads. This guide covers the formula and a worked example for each one, including the mode-specific terms — unimodal, bimodal, no mode — that trip people up most often.

Work through these four calculations in order on the same sorted list, and you will have every measure in a couple of minutes.

Treat this as a repeatable routine, not four separate tricks — the first step (sorting) does most of the work for three of the four measures. Once you've run it on a few datasets by hand, the pattern becomes automatic enough that you'll start spotting the mode and median positions while you sort, before you even reach for a calculator.

## What Are Mean, Median, Mode and Range?

Mean, median, and mode are measures of central tendency: each one answers "what's typical here?" in its own way. Range is a measure of spread: it answers "how far apart are the extremes?" The table below lines up what each one measures and how you get it.

No single measure wins every time. A dataset with one extreme value can make the mean misleading while the median stays informative, and a list of favorite colors has no numerical center at all, which is exactly where the mode becomes the only usable measure. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods' chapter on measures of location](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm) defines the mean, median, and mode as the standard set of "location" statistics and notes that more than one is often reported side by side precisely because each behaves differently on the same data.

Reporting all four together also protects you from a specific failure mode: two datasets that look identical on one measure can be very different datasets. A class where every student scored close to 75 and a class where half scored near 50 and half near 100 can both land on a mean of 75, but the median, mode, and range would immediately show they are not the same distribution. That is the practical reason this guide walks through all four rather than just the one you were asked for.

## How Do You Calculate Mean, Median and Mode?

**Mean.** Add every value, then divide by the count of values: mean = (sum of all values) ÷ n. For the set 4, 8, 6, 10, 12, the sum is 40 and n is 5, so the mean is 40 ÷ 5 = 8. The full step-by-step method, including what happens when an outlier enters the set, is in Statohub's <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-mean')}>guide to finding the mean</Link>.

**Median.** Sort the values first. With an odd count, the median sits at position (n + 1) / 2 in the sorted list; with an even count, it's the average of the two middle values. For 3, 7, 9, 12, 15 (five values, odd, n = 5), position = (5 + 1) / 2 = 3rd value, so the median is 9. For 2, 6, 10, 14 (four values, even), the median is (6 + 10) ÷ 2 = 8. [OpenStax's Introductory Statistics](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/2-5-measures-of-the-center-of-the-data) works through this same odd/even position split with its own worked examples, and Statohub's <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-median')}>median guide</Link> walks through grouped data and ties in more depth.

**Mode.** List every distinct value, count how many times each one appears, and pick the value (or values) with the highest count. Unlike the mean and median, this works without doing any arithmetic on the numbers themselves — it only needs you to count repeats. The next two sections cover exactly how.

Spreadsheet software follows the same logic. Excel computes the median by finding the number in the middle of a set of numbers, and if the set has an even count, [Microsoft's own documentation for the MEDIAN function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/median-function-d0916313-4753-414c-8537-ce85bdd967d2) confirms it averages the two middle numbers — the identical rule you'd apply by hand. Excel splits its mode functions in two (one for the single most common value, another for cases with ties), which is a hint that "the mode" isn't always a single clean number.

## What Is Mode in Math?

The mode is the value that appears most often in a dataset. It's the only one of the three central measures that doesn't require numbers at all — it works just as well on a list of favorite subjects or blood types as it does on test scores, because all it measures is which outcome repeats the most.

What does mode mean in math, more precisely? Per the [NIST/SEMATECH handbook's definition](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm), "the mode is the value of the random sample that occurs with the greatest frequency. It is not necessarily unique." That second sentence matters: a dataset can tie for most-frequent value, and when it does, there's more than one mode. [Wolfram MathWorld's entry on the mode](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Mode.html) gives the plainest version: the mode of a set of observations is simply the most commonly occurring value, found by counting, not calculating.

<Callout variant="quote" source="NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods" sourceHref="https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm">
The mode is the value of the random sample that occurs with the greatest frequency. It is not necessarily unique.
</Callout>

Continuous data complicates the mode slightly, because a measurement like height or reaction time rarely repeats exactly twice. The [NIST/SEMATECH handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm) handles this by treating the mode qualitatively: it describes a dataset's shape by how many dominant "humps" show up in a histogram, and for continuous measurements it suggests using the midpoint of the histogram's tallest class interval as a more meaningful stand-in for an exact mode. That is why you'll sometimes see "modal class" instead of "mode" in a report on continuous, grouped data.

## How Do You Find Mode in Math When a Dataset Has One, None, or Several?

Finding the mode always starts the same way: list each distinct value, tally how often it appears, and compare the counts. What happens next depends on how those counts come out.

**One mode (unimodal).** One value's count beats every other value's count outright.
Dataset: 2, 3, 3, 5, 7 → the value 3 appears twice, more than any other → mode = 3.

**No mode.** Every value appears exactly once, so there's no "most frequent" value to point to.
Dataset: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 → every count is 1 → mode = none (sometimes written "no mode" or "mode undefined").

**More than one mode (bimodal or multimodal).** Two or more values tie for the top count.
Dataset: 2, 2, 3, 5, 5, 7 → both 2 and 5 appear twice → modes = 2 and 5 (this dataset is bimodal). [Wolfram MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Mode.html) labels this pattern bimodal, trimodal, or more generally multimodal once three or more values tie.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
For any dataset longer than about ten values, build a frequency table instead of eyeballing the list: write each distinct value once, then tally every time it repeats. Statohub's <Link to={routes.article('frequency-table')}>frequency table guide</Link> covers the same tallying method for grouped and continuous data.
</Callout>

## How Does the Range Fit In?

The range measures spread, not center: it's the largest value in a dataset minus the smallest. The [NIST/SEMATECH handbook's chapter on measures of scale](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda356.htm) defines it exactly that way — "the range is the largest value minus the smallest value in a data set" — and flags the same limitation every introductory course teaches: the range depends only on the two most extreme values and captures nothing about how the data behaves near the center.

For the set 5, 12, 3, 19, 8, the maximum is 19 and the minimum is 3, so the range is 19 − 3 = 16. That number says nothing about whether the other three values cluster tightly or spread evenly between 3 and 19 — which is exactly why a single large or small observation can make the range look far bigger than the dataset's "typical" spread. Test scores illustrate the same point: a class where scores run from 40 to 100 has a range of 60 whether 29 students scored in the 90s and one scored a 40, or the scores are spread evenly across the whole band — the range alone can't tell those two classes apart. For a spread measure that doesn't throw away every value except the two extremes, the standard deviation is built from every value's distance from the mean instead: the [NIST/SEMATECH handbook's measures-of-scale chapter](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda356.htm) defines it as the square root of the average squared distance from the mean. [OpenStax's Introductory Statistics](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/2-7-measures-of-the-spread-of-the-data) covers this same family of spread measures, including variance and standard deviation, in more depth. Statohub's <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-range')}>range guide</Link> covers grouped data and negative values if you need the range specifically rather than a fuller spread measure like <Link to={routes.article('variance')}>variance</Link> or <Link to={routes.article('standard-deviation')}>standard deviation</Link>.

## Which Measure Should You Report: Mean, Median, or Mode?

The right measure of center depends on what the data looks like and what you're about to do with the number, not on habit.

<DataTable
  caption="Choosing between mean, median, and mode"
  headers={['Situation', 'Best measure', 'Why']}
  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[
    ['Symmetric numeric data, no extreme values', 'Mean', 'Uses every value and stays mathematically convenient for further calculations.'],
    ['Skewed data or data with outliers (income, wait times)', 'Median', 'Depends only on position, so one extreme value barely moves it.'],
    ['Categorical or non-numeric data', 'Mode', 'The only one of the three that works without an ordering or arithmetic.'],
    ['Ranked/ordinal categories (survey scales)', 'Median or mode', 'An arithmetic average does not make conceptual sense on ranks.'],
  ]}
/>

Outliers show exactly why this choice matters. Take 2, 6, 10, 14: the mean is (2 + 6 + 10 + 14) / 4 = 8, and the median is (6 + 10) ÷ 2 = 8 — the same number. Now replace the 14 with 1000, giving 2, 6, 10, 1000: the median is still (6 + 10) ÷ 2 = 8, barely touched, while the mean jumps to (2 + 6 + 10 + 1000) / 4 = 254.5 — more than 30 times its original value. One extreme value left the median unchanged and moved the mean dramatically.

Government income statistics make the same choice for the same reason. The [U.S. Census Bureau's headline income figure](https://www.census.gov/topics/income-poverty/income.html) is median household income, not mean household income. Statohub's own read on why: a relatively small number of very high incomes would pull a mean upward and overstate what a typical household earns, the same way the single value of 1000 pulled the mean above in the example — the median stays anchored to where most households actually sit.

Whichever measure of center you report, pair it with a measure of spread. Two datasets can share the same mean of 50 while one has a range of 5 and the other a range of 80; without the spread figure, those datasets would look identical when they are not.

## A Worked Example: Finding All Four Measures From One Dataset

**Dataset:** 3, 5, 5, 7, 9, 11, 11, 11, 13, 15 (10 values, already sorted)

1. **Confirm the data is sorted.** 3, 5, 5, 7, 9, 11, 11, 11, 13, 15 — already ascending, so no further sorting is needed.
2. **Calculate the mean.**
   ```
   Sum = 3 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 + 11 + 11 + 13 + 15 = 90
   Mean = 90 / 10 = 9
   ```
3. **Find the median.** With 10 values (even), average the 5th and 6th values in the sorted list: the 5th value is 9 and the 6th is 11.
   ```
   Median = (9 + 11) / 2 = 10
   ```
4. **Identify the mode.** Tally each value's frequency: 3 appears 1 time, 5 appears 2 times, 7 appears 1 time, 9 appears 1 time, 11 appears 3 times, 13 appears 1 time, 15 appears 1 time. The value 11 has the highest count.
   ```
   Mode = 11
   ```
5. **Calculate the range.**
   ```
   Range = 15 − 3 = 12
   ```

Reading the four results together: mean = 9, median = 10, mode = 11, range = 12. The mode sits above both the median and the mean because three values cluster at 11, pulling that count higher than any single value below it. [Wolfram MathWorld's entry on the mode](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Mode.html) notes an approximate empirical ordering between the mean, median, and mode for unimodal distributions of moderate asymmetry — not a strict rule. [OpenStax's chapter on skewness and the mean, median, and mode](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/2-6-skewness-and-the-mean-median-and-mode) covers the same relationship and is likewise careful not to treat it as universal, so treat the gap between your own mean, median, and mode as a clue about shape, not as proof of it.

The decision for this dataset: the mean (9) and median (10) sit only 1 apart against a range of 12, which is a small gap relative to the spread, so either one is a fair summary here — report the mean alongside the range (12) rather than alone, and note the mode (11) separately since it is pulled up by the repeated value.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Compute the gap between your mean and median before you report either one. A small gap suggests a fairly symmetric dataset where the mean is safe to use; a large gap is a signal to lean on the median (and check for outliers) instead.
</Callout>

## What Mistakes Do People Often Make With Mean, Median, Mode and Range?

- **Finding the median without sorting first.** The median formula only works on an ordered list — picking the "middle" value from an unsorted list gives a meaningless number.
- **Treating "no mode" as if it were zero.** A dataset where every value is unique has no mode at all; it does not have a mode of 0, and writing "0" there misrepresents the data.
- **Averaging two already-averaged numbers.** Combining two group means by averaging the means themselves ignores how many observations each group had; see Statohub's <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-mean')}>mean guide</Link> for the weighted fix.
- **Reporting a measure of center without a spread measure, or trusting the range as the whole spread story.** A mean, median, or mode without an accompanying spread measure (the range, at minimum) tells only half the story, and the range itself can mislead because it uses only the two most extreme values — one unusual observation can make it look far larger than the data's typical spread. The [NIST/SEMATECH box plot chapter](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/boxplot.htm) shows how a box plot flags that kind of outlier before you trust the range on its own.
- **Picking the mean out of habit, even with outliers present.** The mean moves with every value in the dataset, so a single very large or very small number can drag it away from what's actually typical; check for outliers before defaulting to the mean.
- **Averaging a bimodal dataset's two modes into one number.** If a dataset has modes of 2 and 5, the mode is "2 and 5," not 3.5 — averaging the modes together produces a number that may not even appear in the original data.

## Statohub's Take on Mean, Median, Mode and Range

Statohub's position is that these four numbers are worth computing together, not in isolation, because the gaps between them are informative. A mean and median that sit close together tell you the data is roughly symmetric; a wide gap, or a mode that sits off to one side, is a cheap and fast signal to look for skew or outliers before you build anything more complex on top of the data. None of the four replaces careful inspection of the actual values, but together they're the fastest honest first read a dataset gets. Treat them as a starting checklist rather than a final answer: once the mean, median, mode, and range disagree with each other, that disagreement is the cue to look at the data directly — a histogram or a sorted list — before choosing which single number, if any, to report onward.
