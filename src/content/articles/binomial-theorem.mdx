The **binomial theorem** is the formula that expands any power of a two-term expression — a binomial such as (a + b) — into a sum of individual terms, without multiplying the expression by itself over and over. For (a + b)ⁿ, it states that the expansion equals the sum, over k = 0 up through n, of C(n, k) times a raised to (n − k) times b raised to k, where C(n, k) is the binomial coefficient and n is any non-negative integer. Those coefficients are exactly the numbers in row n of Pascal's triangle, so you can read them straight off the triangle instead of computing a single factorial. The result always has n + 1 terms, running from a raised to the full power n down to b raised to the full power n.

## What Is the Binomial Theorem?

The binomial theorem states that for any real numbers a and b and any non-negative integer n, (a + b)ⁿ expands into a sum of n + 1 terms, each of the form C(n, k)·a^(n−k)·b^k, with k running from 0 to n. Written out:

```
(a + b)^n = C(n,0)·a^n        + C(n,1)·a^(n-1)·b
          + C(n,2)·a^(n-2)·b^2 + ... + C(n,n)·b^n
```

The first term carries a to the full power n and b to the power 0; each later term shifts one power from a to b; the last term carries b to the full power n. NIST's Digital Library of Mathematical Functions states the same identity in its section on binomial coefficients, where it defines the coefficient and the expansion together as one of the handbook's core algebraic formulas — see the [NIST DLMF, §1.2 Elementary Algebra](https://dlmf.nist.gov/1.2). [OpenStax Precalculus 2e](https://openstax.org/books/precalculus-2e/pages/11-6-binomial-theorem) states the same sum formula in its chapter on the binomial theorem, alongside the same Pascal's-triangle shortcut for reading off the coefficients.

A **binomial** is simply any expression with exactly two terms — a + b, x − y, 2p + 3 — and the prefix *bi-* signals that count. The coefficient on each term counts the number of ways to choose which k of the n factors of (a + b) contribute a b instead of an a. Each of the n factors makes an independent a-or-b choice, the same independent-choice structure covered by the <Link to={routes.article('fundamental-counting-principle')}>fundamental counting principle</Link>; the binomial theorem is what happens once you tally up every way those n independent choices can land.

## How Do You Calculate a Binomial Coefficient?

A binomial coefficient C(n, k) — read "n choose k" — counts the number of ways to pick k items out of n without caring about order, and its value is n! divided by k! times (n − k)!.

```
C(n, k) = n! / ( k! · (n - k)! )

C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! · 2!) = 24 / 4 = 6
```

Order doesn't matter here: choosing factors 1 and 3 of (a + b)⁴ to supply the b is the same selection as choosing factors 3 and 1, which is exactly why this is a combination count and not a permutation count — see <Link to={routes.article('permutations-and-combinations')}>permutations vs. combinations</Link> for the general rule that separates the two, or <Link to={routes.article('combinations')}>the combination formula</Link> for the full derivation of C(n, k) on its own.

Two properties matter most for the binomial theorem specifically: C(n, 0) = C(n, n) = 1 (there's exactly one way to choose nothing, and exactly one way to choose everything), and C(n, k) = C(n, n − k), since choosing k items to include is the same act as choosing n − k items to leave out.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
That symmetry cuts your work roughly in half. For (a + b)⁶, once you know C(6,0) through C(6,3) are 1, 6, 15, 20, the remaining C(6,4), C(6,5), C(6,6) are just the mirror image: 15, 6, 1.
</Callout>

To compute C(n, k) by hand or verify a row of Pascal's triangle, the <Link to={routes.calculator('combination')}>combination calculator</Link> runs the factorial arithmetic for you.

## How Does Pascal's Triangle Generate the Binomial Coefficients?

Pascal's triangle is a triangular array of numbers in which every interior entry equals the sum of the two entries directly above it, and row n of the triangle lists C(n, 0) through C(n, n) in order — exactly the coefficients the binomial theorem needs for (a + b)ⁿ.

```
Row 0:              1
Row 1:            1   1
Row 2:          1   2   1
Row 3:        1   3   3   1
Row 4:      1   4   6   4   1
Row 5:    1   5  10  10   5   1
Row 6:  1   6  15  20  15   6   1
```

For (a + b)⁴, row 4 gives 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 — the same five coefficients the factorial formula produces for C(4,0) through C(4,4). To build row 5 from row 4, place a 1 at each end, then sum each adjacent pair above it: 1+4 = 5, 4+6 = 10, 6+4 = 10, 4+1 = 5, giving 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1.

### Why Each Entry Is a Sum of the Two Above It

The rule — each entry equals the sum of its two parents — is **Pascal's identity**:

```
C(n, k) = C(n-1, k-1) + C(n-1, k)
```

The combinatorial reason is a fixed-item argument. Pick one specific factor out of the n factors of (a + b) and call it the "marked" one. Any way of choosing k factors to supply a b either includes the marked factor or it doesn't: if it does, you still need to choose k − 1 more b's from the remaining n − 1 factors (that's C(n−1, k−1) ways); if it doesn't, you need to choose all k b's from the remaining n − 1 factors (that's C(n−1, k) ways). Adding those two mutually exclusive cases gives every way to choose k b's from n factors, which is exactly C(n, k). NIST's handbook frames this same recurrence through lattice-path counting in its chapter on combinatorial analysis — see the [NIST DLMF, §26.3 Lattice Paths: Binomial Coefficients](https://dlmf.nist.gov/26.3).

That same fixed-item logic is why the small cases work out the way they do. For (a + b)² = (a + b)(a + b), each factor independently contributes an a or a b, giving a², ab, ba, and b² before combining like terms — the single ab-type term appears twice, so its coefficient is 2 = C(2,1), matching row 2 of the triangle.

<Callout variant="quote" source="MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews — Blaise Pascal biography" sourceHref="https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Pascal/">
Although Pascal was not the first to study the Pascal triangle, his work on the topic in Treatise on the Arithmetical Triangle was the most important on this topic and, through the work of Wallis, Pascal's work on the binomial coefficients was to lead Newton to his discovery of the general binomial theorem for fractional and negative powers.
</Callout>

Pascal's *Treatise on the Arithmetical Triangle* is the work the quote above credits, and the pattern had already been studied by earlier mathematicians elsewhere; what made Pascal's treatment lasting was tying the triangle explicitly to the binomial coefficients and to the emerging theory of probability. That link to Newton's later work matters for this article too — it resurfaces below when the theorem is extended past whole-number powers.

For small n, reading a row off the triangle is faster than running the factorial formula k + 1 times; for large n or symbolic work, the factorial formula C(n, k) = n! / (k!(n − k)!) is easier to apply directly without building six or seven rows by hand.

## How Do You Expand a Binomial Step by Step?

Expanding (a + b)ⁿ comes down to four repeatable steps: identify a, b, and n; list the coefficients C(n, 0) through C(n, n); write each term C(n, k)·a^(n−k)·b^k for k = 0 through n; then simplify the numbers.

Each step narrows the work. Step 1 fixes what you're plugging in, so a mislabeled a and b cannot propagate through the rest of the calculation. Step 2 produces a short list of n + 1 numbers you can double-check against Pascal's triangle before writing a single term. Step 3 is mechanical once the coefficients are in hand — the exponents on a and b always sum to n, so a quick check that each term's exponents add up correctly catches most transcription errors immediately. Step 4 is where constants get multiplied together and like powers get combined into the final polynomial.

The <Link to={routes.calculator('factorial')}>factorial calculator</Link> is useful here if you'd rather verify n! by hand before cancelling it against (n − k)!.

### Handling a Minus Sign: Expanding (x − 3)³

When the second term is negative, rewrite (a − b)ⁿ as (a + (−b))ⁿ and apply the same four steps — the only change is that powers of (−b) alternate in sign. Take a = x, b = −3, n = 3:

```
k=0: C(3,0) · x^3 · (-3)^0 = 1 · x^3 · 1    =  x^3
k=1: C(3,1) · x^2 · (-3)^1 = 3 · x^2 · (-3) = -9x^2
k=2: C(3,2) · x^1 · (-3)^2 = 3 · x   · 9    = 27x
k=3: C(3,3) · x^0 · (-3)^3 = 1 · 1   · (-27)= -27
```

So (x − 3)³ = x³ − 9x² + 27x − 27. The signs alternate because odd powers of −3 are negative and even powers are positive — that pattern holds for every (a − b)ⁿ expansion, which is why the checklist above calls out tracking (−1)^k separately from the numeric magnitude.

## How Is the Binomial Theorem Different From the Binomial Distribution?

The binomial theorem is an algebraic identity that expands (a + b)ⁿ into a polynomial; the binomial distribution is a probability model that uses the same coefficient to compute the chance of exactly k successes in n independent trials. They share the name and the coefficient C(n, k), but they produce different kinds of answers.

If you need to expand (2x + 3)⁵ into a polynomial, that's the binomial theorem. If you need the probability of exactly 3 heads in 5 fair coin flips, that's the binomial distribution, which [OpenStax Introductory Statistics](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/4-3-binomial-distribution) defines over a fixed number of independent trials with the same success probability p on each trial. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm) gives the same P(X = k) formula along with its mean (np) and standard deviation (√(np(1 − p))) for that distribution. For the full probability treatment — mean, variance, and when the binomial distribution applies — see <Link to={routes.article('binomial-distribution')}>binomial distribution: formula and examples</Link>.

## What Patterns and Extensions Does the Binomial Theorem Have?

A handful of substitutions and special cases turn the general formula into quick identities worth recognizing on sight.

Setting a = b = 1 collapses the formula to the sum of a full row of Pascal's triangle:

```
(1 + 1)^n = Σ C(n, k) = 2^n
```

Row 4 sums to 1 + 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 = 16 = 2⁴, which matches the combinatorial fact that a 4-element set has 2⁴ = 16 subsets in total, including the empty set and the full set. Setting a = 1, b = −1 instead gives the alternating sum of any row (for n ≥ 1):

```
(1 - 1)^n = 0 = Σ (-1)^k · C(n, k)
```

Row 4 again: 1 − 4 + 6 − 4 + 1 = 0. That alternating-sum identity underlies inclusion–exclusion arguments throughout combinatorics and probability.

For even n, the single largest coefficient sits in the middle of the row, at C(n, n/2). Row 6's middle entry, C(6, 3) = 20, is larger than any of its neighbors, and that same middle-heavy, symmetric shape is what makes the <Link to={routes.article('binomial-distribution')}>binomial distribution</Link>'s probability bars look bell-shaped for large n — the coefficients are the same numbers driving both pictures.

**Finding one term without expanding everything.** The (r + 1)-th term of (a + b)ⁿ is C(n, r)·a^(n−r)·b^r, so you can isolate a single term by exponent instead of writing out the whole expansion. To find the term containing x² in (x + 3)⁵, set b = x and look for b raised to the power 2, so r = 2:

```
T(3) = C(5, 2) · 3^(5-2) · x^2 = 10 · 27 · x^2 = 270x^2
```

**Beyond two terms.** The same lattice-path counting argument extends past binomials: expanding (a + b + c)ⁿ distributes the n factors across three outcomes instead of two, producing multinomial coefficients that generalize C(n, k) — covered in the [NIST DLMF, §26.4 Multinomial Coefficients](https://dlmf.nist.gov/26.4).

**Beyond whole-number powers.** The plain binomial theorem only covers non-negative integer n, where the sum terminates after n + 1 terms. Isaac Newton extended it to an infinite series valid for any real exponent, provided the second term is small enough for the series to converge:

```
(1 + x)^a = 1 + ax + a(a-1)/2! · x^2 + a(a-1)(a-2)/3! · x^3 + ...   for |x| < 1
```

[OpenStax Calculus Volume 2](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-4-working-with-taylor-series) covers this generalized binomial series as part of writing Taylor series for functions like (1 + x)^(1/2). Truncating that infinite series after its first two terms gives the approximation (1 + x)^a ≈ 1 + ax, which engineers and physicists lean on whenever x is small enough that the higher-order terms barely move the answer — a shortcut that only works because the series converges for |x| < 1 in the first place. The connection to Pascal runs through this same history: as the MacTutor Pascal biography quoted above states, it was Pascal's work on binomial coefficients, carried forward through Wallis, that led Newton to the fractional- and negative-exponent version.

## A Worked Example: Expanding (x + 2)⁴

Expand (x + 2)⁴ using the binomial theorem, with a = x, b = 2, n = 4.

1. **Identify the pieces.** a = x, b = 2, n = 4.
2. **List the coefficients.** Row 4 of Pascal's triangle gives C(4,0)=1, C(4,1)=4, C(4,2)=6, C(4,3)=4, C(4,4)=1.
3. **Write each term:**

```
k=0: C(4,0) · x^4 · 2^0 = 1 · x^4 · 1  =  x^4
k=1: C(4,1) · x^3 · 2^1 = 4 · x^3 · 2  =  8x^3
k=2: C(4,2) · x^2 · 2^2 = 6 · x^2 · 4  = 24x^2
k=3: C(4,3) · x^1 · 2^3 = 4 · x   · 8  = 32x
k=4: C(4,4) · x^0 · 2^4 = 1 · 1   · 16 = 16
```

4. **Simplify and combine:**

```
(x + 2)^4 = x^4 + 8x^3 + 24x^2 + 32x + 16
```

**Verify each coefficient:** the x³ coefficient is C(4,1)×2¹ = 4×2 = 8; the x² coefficient is C(4,2)×2² = 6×4 = 24; the x coefficient is C(4,3)×2³ = 4×8 = 32; the constant term is C(4,4)×2⁴ = 1×16 = 16. All four match the expansion above.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Run an x = 1 check on any expansion before you trust it. Plugging x = 1 into the expanded polynomial gives 1 + 8 + 24 + 32 + 16 = 81, and the original expression at x = 1 gives (1 + 2)⁴ = 3⁴ = 81 — the totals agree, which confirms no term was dropped or miscomputed. (This is different from the coefficient-sum check in the checklist above, which adds only the C(n, k) values and compares against 2ⁿ.)
</Callout>

## Common Mistakes With the Binomial Theorem

- **Forgetting to raise b to its power.** In C(n, k)·a^(n−k)·b^k, the b must be raised to k, not left as a bare coefficient. In (x + 2)⁴, the k = 2 term is C(4,2) × 2² = 6 × 4 = 24, not just 6 — always multiply by the full b^k factor.
- **Dropping the alternating sign in (a − b)ⁿ.** Writing b as (−b) from the start and tracking (−1)^k separately from the magnitude avoids the sign slip that produces a plausible-looking but wrong polynomial.
- **Applying the finite formula to a non-integer n.** The n + 1-term expansion only holds for non-negative integer n. Fractional or negative exponents require Newton's infinite generalized binomial series instead, which converges only when the second term's magnitude is below 1.
- **Confusing C(n, k) with nᵏ.** C(4, 2) = 6, while 4² = 16 — completely different quantities computed by completely different formulas. Reach for n!/(k!(n−k)!) or Pascal's triangle, never a plain exponent.
- **Miscounting the number of terms.** (a + b)ⁿ always has n + 1 terms, not n, because k runs from 0 through n inclusive. Missing either the k = 0 or k = n term is the most common way to come up one term short.

## Statohub's Take on the Binomial Theorem

Statohub's position is that Pascal's triangle should be the default tool for any expansion with n under about 8 or 9 — it's faster, it's visual, and the symmetry is obvious on sight, so errors are easier to catch. Reach for the factorial formula C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n−k)!) once n gets large or you're working symbolically rather than numerically, since building a dozen rows of the triangle by hand stops being faster than it looks. And keep the theorem and the distribution mentally filed apart: one produces a polynomial, the other a probability, and conflating them is the single most common mix-up this topic produces.
