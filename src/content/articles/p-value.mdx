A **p value** is the probability of getting a test result at least as extreme as the one you observed, calculated under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true. A small p value means your data would be unusual if the null hypothesis held, which counts as evidence against it; a large p value means your data are perfectly ordinary under that assumption and gives you no reason to doubt it. So what is p value in statistics? It is not the probability your hypothesis is true, and it is not the probability you made an error — it is a single conditional probability, computed from a test statistic and a sampling distribution, that tells you how surprising your result is.

## What Is a P-Value in Statistics?

A p value measures how compatible your observed data are with a specific null hypothesis, expressed as a probability between 0 and 1. Formally:

```
p = P( test statistic as extreme or more extreme than observed | H₀ is true )
```

"As extreme" means in the direction your alternative hypothesis predicts. For a two-tailed test — where you only care that there is a difference, not its direction — "more extreme" covers both tails of the sampling distribution; for a one-tailed test it covers only the tail your alternative points toward.

[NIST/SEMATECH's handbook chapter on statistical tests](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc13.htm) frames a statistical test as a decision mechanism built on two competing claims: a null hypothesis H₀ and an alternative H₁, with a significance level α set in advance as the acceptable risk of rejecting a true H₀. [OpenStax's Introductory Statistics](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/9-1-null-and-alternative-hypotheses) defines H₀ as the claim assumed true unless the data say otherwise, and H₁ as the claim you are testing for — the <Link to={routes.article('null-hypothesis')}>null hypothesis article</Link> covers how to state each one correctly. The p value is the number that connects your sample to that decision: it is computed from the test statistic, and a p value near 0 says results like yours would be rare under H₀, while a p value near 1 says they would be common.

For a quick mental anchor: flip a coin 20 times and get 15 heads. If the coin were fair, 15 or more heads out of 20 would occur by chance alone about 2% of the time. That 2% is the p value for "is this coin fair?" — small enough that most analysts would start doubting the coin.

## What Is the P-Value Formula?

The p-value formula depends on which test statistic you compute, but the logic is identical in every case: convert your sample result into a standardized statistic, then find the tail area of that statistic's sampling distribution.

Use the **z formula** only when the population standard deviation σ is known from long-run process data; otherwise use the **t formula**, substituting the sample standard deviation s and looking up the tail area in the Student's t-distribution instead of the standard normal. [NIST/SEMATECH's table of the standard normal CDF](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3671.htm) and its [table of Student's t critical values](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3672.htm) are the reference tables behind each row above. The same tail-area logic extends to proportions, chi-square statistics, and F statistics — only the sampling distribution changes; see <Link to={routes.article('chi-square-test')}>the chi-square test</Link> and its underlying <Link to={routes.article('chi-square-distribution')}>chi-square distribution</Link> for a worked case that uses the same p-value mechanics on a different statistic.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
The larger the test statistic's absolute value, the farther it sits from zero, and the smaller the tail area beyond it — so a bigger |z| or |t| always produces a smaller p value, never the reverse.
</Callout>

## How Do You Calculate a P-Value From a Z-Score or T-Statistic?

How to calculate p value comes down to three mechanical moves: compute the statistic, find the one-tail area, then adjust for the number of tails your test needs.

**From a z-score.** Compute z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n), then look up the cumulative probability at that z in a standard normal table — the <Link to={routes.article('z-score')}>z-score article</Link> covers that standardization in more depth, and the <Link to={routes.calculator('z-table')}>z-table calculator</Link> returns the cumulative probability directly. Subtract the result from 1 to get the right-tail area, and double it for a two-tailed test.

**From a t-statistic.** Compute t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n) with df = n − 1, the same ratio the <Link to={routes.article('t-test')}>t-test article</Link> derives in full, including why the t-distribution's heavier tails produce a larger p value than a z-test would give the same numeric statistic in a small sample. Look up the tail area for that t and df in a t-table, or let software do it (see below).

**From other statistics.** A chi-square statistic, an F statistic from ANOVA, or a rank-sum statistic from a nonparametric test all produce a p value the same way — the <Link to={routes.article('test-statistic')}>test statistic guide</Link> covers how degrees of freedom and table lookups generalize across these families.

## A Worked Example: Two-Tailed Z-Test for a Filling Machine

A quality-control team wants to know whether the mean weight of cereal boxes leaving a filling machine differs from the target of **500 g** — the same "is the process mean still on target" question [NIST/SEMATECH's handbook on comparisons to an assumed process mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc22.htm) walks through for one-sample tests. Historical process data give a known standard deviation of **σ = 9 g**. The team samples **n = 36** boxes and measures a sample mean of **x̄ = 503 g**.

  <ProcessFlow

    desc="Four connected steps: state the hypotheses and set alpha; compute the standard error and z statistic; find the tail area and double it for the two-tailed p-value; compare p to alpha and decide."
    steps={[

      { label: 'Compute SE and z', detail: 'SE = 9/√36 = 1.5; z = (503−500)/1.5 = 2.0.' },
      { label: 'Find the tail area', detail: 'P(Z>2.0) = 0.0228; two-tailed p = 0.0456.' },
      { label: 'Compare and decide', detail: 'p = 0.046 < α = 0.05 → reject H₀.' },
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

1. **State the hypotheses and significance level.**

```
H₀: μ = 500 g
H₁: μ ≠ 500 g   (two-tailed)
α = 0.05, chosen before collecting data
```

The team uses a two-tailed test because boxes that are too light or too heavy are both a problem.

2. **Compute the standard error and the test statistic.**

```
SE = σ / √n = 9 / √36 = 9 / 6 = 1.5 g

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / SE = (503 − 500) / 1.5 = 3 / 1.5 = 2.0
```

3. **Find the p-value.** The area to the right of z = 2.0 under the standard normal curve is 0.0228. Because the test is two-tailed, both tails count:

```
P(Z > 2.0) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228

p = 2 × 0.0228 = 0.0456
```

4. **Compare p to α and decide.**

```
p = 0.0456 < α = 0.05  →  reject H₀
```

The team concludes the mean fill weight is statistically significantly different from 500 g at the 5% level and flags the machine for inspection.

If the team only cared about boxes running *heavy* — a directional alternative, H₁: μ > 500 g — the test would be one-tailed instead, and only the right tail would count:

```
p = P(Z > 2.0) = 0.0228  ≈  0.023   (one-tailed, right)
```

That one-tailed p value is about half the two-tailed one, because it spends the whole α budget on a single direction instead of splitting it across both. Picking one tail after looking at the data would invalidate the test; the <Link to={routes.article('one-tailed-vs-two-tailed')}>one-tailed vs. two-tailed guide</Link> covers how to commit to a tail in advance and when a directional H₁ is actually justified.

For a one-sample t-test with a smaller sample, the mechanics are identical with a different distribution. Take n = 15 matched-pair differences with a sample mean difference of x̄ = 2.3 and sample standard deviation s = 4.1, testing against μ₀ = 0:

```
SE = s / √n = 4.1 / √15 = 1.059

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / SE = 2.3 / 1.059 = 2.173,  df = 14

two-tailed p ≈ 0.047
```

Because 0.047 is still below 0.05, this result also clears the conventional threshold. The t-distribution's heavier tails do make a real difference, though: holding the statistic fixed at exactly 2.0 instead, a z-test gives a two-tailed p of 0.0455, while a t-test with df = 14 gives a two-tailed p of about 0.065 — noticeably larger for the identical numeric statistic, because the t-distribution puts more probability into its tails at low degrees of freedom.

## How Do You Find a P-Value in Excel, Python, or R?

Software removes the table-lookup step, but you still need to compute the right statistic first.

```
Excel:   For a two-sample or paired comparison, =T.TEST(array1, array2, tails, type)
         returns the p-value directly. For a z-based one-sample test, compute z by
         hand, then use =NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE) to get the cumulative probability and
         derive the tail area from it.

Python:  from scipy import stats
         stats.ttest_1samp(sample, popmean=mu0)   # returns (statistic, pvalue)
         stats.norm.sf(z)                         # one-tailed p from a z-statistic

R:       t.test(sample, mu = mu0)        # one-sample t-test, reports the p-value
         2 * pnorm(-abs(z))              # two-tailed p from a z-statistic
```

[SciPy's `ttest_1samp` reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_1samp.html) documents the one-sample t-test function used above, and [SciPy's `scipy.stats.norm` reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.norm.html) documents the normal-distribution methods, including the survival function used for a one-tailed z p-value. Microsoft's documentation for [`T.TEST`](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/t-test-function-d4e08ec3-c545-485f-962e-276f7cbed055) and [`NORM.S.DIST`](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/norm-s-dist-function-1e787282-3832-4520-a9ae-bd2a8d99ba88) covers the Excel syntax above, including the `tails` and `type` arguments that select one- vs. two-tailed and paired vs. independent designs.

## How Do You Choose a Significance Level to Compare Against?

A p value only becomes a decision once you compare it to a pre-chosen significance level α — the maximum p value you are willing to treat as evidence against H₀. NIST/SEMATECH describes α as the risk you accept of rejecting a true null hypothesis, and that risk should be set before you see the data, not adjusted afterward to fit a result you want.

```
If p < α  →  reject H₀ (statistically significant at level α)
If p ≥ α  →  fail to reject H₀ (not enough evidence against H₀)
```

"Fail to reject" is not "prove true." It only means the sample did not produce enough evidence to overturn the null hypothesis — the <Link to={routes.article('null-hypothesis')}>null hypothesis article</Link> covers why that asymmetry exists and why H₀ can never be "proven" by a test, only rejected or not rejected.

The international [ICH E9 guideline on statistical principles for clinical trials](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/E9_Guideline.pdf) — the standard regulators use to evaluate drug trials — states that "conventionally the probability of type I error is set at 5% or less," which is the source of the 0.05 row above. At the opposite extreme, particle physicists require evidence equivalent to a five-standard-deviation ("5σ") result before claiming a discovery, a convention [discussed in Louis Lyons' review of open statistical issues in particle physics](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.1663); a one-tailed 5σ result corresponds to a p value of about 3 × 10⁻⁷, several orders of magnitude stricter than the 0.05 most fields use. The stricter the threshold, the more a field is weighting the cost of a false positive against the cost of missing a real effect — a trade-off the <Link to={routes.article('type-i-and-type-ii-errors')}>Type I and Type II errors guide</Link> and the <Link to={routes.article('statistical-power')}>statistical power article</Link> cover from the other side.

## Common Mistakes When Interpreting a P-Value

- **Treating the p-value as the probability H₀ is true.** The p value is computed *assuming* H₀, so it cannot also tell you the probability that assumption is correct. The [American Statistical Association's statement on statistical significance and p-values](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/P-ValueStatement.pdf) states this directly: "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone." Getting that probability requires a prior and Bayes' theorem, which the standard calculation never supplies.
- **Equating statistical significance with practical importance.** A large enough sample can push p below 0.001 for an effect too small to matter in practice. Report an effect size — Cohen's d, a correlation, an odds ratio — alongside the p value; the <Link to={routes.article('effect-size')}>effect size guide</Link> covers how to compute and interpret the common ones.
- **Reading a non-significant result as proof of no effect.** Failing to reject H₀ can mean the effect doesn't exist, or it can mean your sample lacked the power to detect a real but smaller effect — a distinction the <Link to={routes.article('statistical-power')}>statistical power article</Link> walks through.
- **Letting a bigger sample size stand in for a bigger effect.** Collecting more data shrinks the standard error and therefore the p value for almost any non-zero effect, which is exactly why sample sizes should be planned in advance rather than inflated until p drops below α.
- **Treating p = 0.05 as a hard pass/fail line.** By the strict rule p < α, a p value of exactly 0.05 with α = 0.05 does not clear the threshold. The ASA statement's principle on full reporting holds that "proper inference requires full reporting and transparency" — reporting the exact p value alongside the decision is more informative than collapsing it to "significant" or "not significant."

<Callout variant="quote" source="American Statistical Association, Statement on Statistical Significance and P-Values (2016)" sourceHref="https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/P-ValueStatement.pdf">
Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.
</Callout>

## Statohub's Take on P-Values

Statohub's position is that a p value is a useful filter, not a verdict. Treat it as one input into a decision that should also include the effect size, the sample size, and whether the result replicates — never as a single number that settles the question on its own. The 0.05 convention is a shared language for comparing studies, not a law of nature, and the honest move is always to report the exact p value rather than just which side of α it landed on.
