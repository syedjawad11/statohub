The synthetic control method constructs a weighted combination of untreated units to form a transparent counterfactual for a treated aggregate unit. We recommend it when a single large unit, a country, region, or city, receives a policy intervention and a long pre-intervention time series exists for that unit and its potential comparators. Its main strength is interpretability: the counterfactual is built from observable data rather than a model's hidden assumptions, and inference typically relies on placebo tests rather than conventional standard errors.

## Overview: What SCM Is and the Assumptions Behind It

We think of synthetic control as building an artificial twin for the unit that received an intervention. Instead of picking one comparison region or country, we assign weights to several untreated "donor" units so their weighted average reproduces the treated unit's pre-intervention trajectory as closely as possible.

In the standard panel-data setup, we observe one treated unit and a donor pool of untreated units over several pre-intervention periods and at least one post-intervention period. The method assumes the treated unit's predictor values can be approximated as a combination of donor values, often described through a convex-hull condition, and that the relationship between predictors and outcomes stays stable across the pre- and post-intervention periods according to the [EU CAP Network's training materials](https://eu-cap-network.ec.europa.eu/training/evaluation-learning-portal/synthetic-control-method_en).

## When SCM Fits and What Data You Need

SCM works best for comparative case studies: a single region adopting a new tax, one country implementing a trade policy, or a handful of large aggregate units exposed to the same shock. It is a poor fit for settings with many treated units or micro-level individual treatment, where other quasi-experimental designs serve better.

Before committing to SCM, we check feasibility against a short list of requirements, since the EU CAP Network stresses that credible synthetic controls depend on long, reliable pre-policy series and genuinely comparable donor units.

- Confirm a pre-intervention series spanning roughly 8 to 10 years or more for aggregate indicators.
- Identify donor units untouched by the intervention and structurally similar to the treated unit.
- Verify predictors are available, consistently measured, and free of major gaps across all units.

## Choosing the Donor Pool and Predictors

The donor pool deserves as much care as the estimation itself. We exclude any unit exposed to the intervention or a closely related policy, since contamination there biases the synthetic control toward the treatment effect it is supposed to measure. We also favor donors sharing structural features with the treated unit, similar industry mix, demographic trends, or institutional context, rather than including every available unit indiscriminately.

Predictors should have real explanatory power over the outcome and must be measured strictly before treatment. We also check whether the treated unit's predictor values fall inside the convex hull formed by the donor pool; when they fall outside it, the fit relies on extrapolation rather than interpolation, which weakens credibility.

- Drop donors affected by the same or a related intervention.
- Select predictors with demonstrated pre-treatment explanatory relevance.
- Test convex-hull membership before finalizing the donor list.
- Address missing data transparently rather than silently dropping periods.

## How SCM Estimates the Weights

The core algorithm chooses a vector of weights, one per donor, that are constrained to be non-negative and to sum to one, so the synthetic control is a convex combination of donor units rather than an unrestricted linear projection. The [AEA's methodological guidance](https://www.aeaweb.org/content/file?id=12409) describes this restriction as the standard safeguard against extrapolation: by forcing weights into a convex combination, the method avoids assigning implausible negative influence to any donor.

The objective function minimizes the distance between the treated unit's pre-intervention predictor and outcome path and the weighted donor path, typically using a generalized distance metric that can weight predictors by their importance. Regression-style analogues relax the non-negativity or sum-to-one constraints, which permits extrapolation but introduces its own bias risks, a trade-off augmented SCM addresses directly. In practice, numerical solvers can struggle when donor predictors are highly collinear, so we recommend checking the optimization's convergence and sensitivity to the chosen predictor set before trusting the resulting weights.

## Testing Significance: Placebo and Permutation Methods

Conventional standard errors and p-values do not transfer well to SCM, because we are usually working with one treated unit and a small, constrained set of weights rather than a large random sample. Research published in the [American Journal of Epidemiology](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/190/12/2700/6336907) confirms that permutation-based inference has become the standard approach precisely because parametric methods perform poorly in these small-N settings.

In practice, we run two placebo exercises: an in-space placebo that reassigns the "treatment" to each donor unit in turn and compares the resulting gaps to the real treated unit's gap, and an in-time placebo that pretends the intervention happened earlier, during a period with no real policy change, and checks whether a false effect appears.

- Report the pre-treatment root mean squared prediction error (RMSPE) for the actual fit.
- Plot the post-treatment gap for the treated unit against the distribution of placebo gaps.
- Present the full donor weight table so readers can judge plausibility.

**A ratio of post-treatment to pre-treatment RMSPE that is large relative to the placebo distribution** is the standard signal that the treated unit's gap is unlikely to have occurred by chance.

## Newer Approaches: Synthetic DiD and Augmented SCM

Synthetic difference-in-differences, introduced by [Arkhangelsky and coauthors](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190159), adds time weights and two-way fixed effects to the classic SCM framework, which tends to improve robustness across a wider range of panel structures. Augmented SCM extends the original method by permitting limited negative weights, which reduces bias when the treated unit sits outside the donor convex hull.

We suggest reaching for these extensions when a straightforward SCM fit fails convex-hull checks or when researchers need inferential tools that approach conventional asymptotic guarantees rather than relying solely on permutation tests.

## Software and a Reproducible Workflow

Several maintained packages support SCM estimation: [`Synth`](https://cran.r-project.org/package=Synth) for R and Stata, `augsynth` and [`scpi`](https://cran.r-project.org/package=scpi) for R, [`gsynth`](https://cran.r-project.org/package=gsynth) for generalized synthetic control, and various Python notebooks replicating the core algorithm. Many published SCM papers also release replication code, which is worth consulting before building an estimator from scratch.

A dependable workflow follows a consistent sequence regardless of the software chosen.

## A Compact Worked Example

Consider a single region that adopts a new regulation in a given year. We would work through the analysis in a fixed order.

1. Define the treated unit and the exact intervention date, then split the panel into pre- and post-periods.
2. Assemble a donor pool of comparable regions unaffected by the regulation and gather predictors measured only before the intervention.
3. Fit the synthetic control, inspect the pre-treatment RMSPE, and review which donors receive positive weight.
4. Compute the post-treatment gap between the treated unit and its synthetic counterpart for each post-intervention period.
5. Run in-space and in-time placebo tests to see whether the observed gap is unusual relative to the placebo distribution.
6. Vary the pre-treatment window and exclude influential donors one at a time to check whether conclusions hold under these sensitivity checks.

Before generalizing, we always weigh the convex-hull position of the treated unit's predictors, since extrapolation beyond the donor pool's range weakens any conclusion drawn from the gap.

## Robustness and a Reporting Checklist

A credible SCM write-up shows its work rather than asserting a clean result. The International Journal of Epidemiology warns that convex-hull violations and "history bias," where an unrelated shock hits treated and donor units simultaneously, are common failure points that silently undermine otherwise careful analyses. We address history bias by testing negative-control outcomes: variables that share the same confounders as the outcome of interest but should be unaffected by the intervention. If placebo gaps appear there too, time-varying confounding is a live concern rather than a remote possibility.

## When SCM Earns Its Keep

We find SCM most convincing when researchers treat it as a transparent case study rather than a universal causal engine: its value comes from showing the data and weights openly, not from mechanically producing a single, definitive number. Subject-matter context, pre-registration where feasible, and shared replication files matter as much as the estimation itself, and sound practice means reporting diagnostics plainly instead of overstating what one donor-weighted comparison can prove. Analysts preparing panel data should also watch for lookahead bias, a pitfall [covered in detail by a partner resource on avoiding lookahead bias in trading data](https://bigmovealgo.com/post/lookahead-bias-trading), since using information unavailable at the time of the intervention can quietly distort pre-treatment fit in the same way it distorts backtested trading signals.
