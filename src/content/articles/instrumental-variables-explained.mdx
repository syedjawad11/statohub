An instrumental variable is a variable that provides quasi-random variation in the treatment so you can estimate a causal effect when the treatment is endogenous. Instrumental variables (IV) regression is the standard remedy when a regressor correlates with the error term, whether through omitted variables, reverse causation, or measurement error. Analysts in economics, epidemiology, and the social sciences reach for IV precisely when a randomized experiment is not feasible but a plausible source of exogenous variation exists.

## Why endogeneity breaks OLS and how an instrument helps

Ordinary least squares assume the regressor is uncorrelated with the error term. That assumption fails in three familiar ways: an omitted variable drives both the treatment and the outcome, the outcome feeds back into the treatment, or the treatment is measured with error. Each case leaves OLS attributing someone else's influence to the variable you care about, producing a biased coefficient that no amount of additional data will correct.

An instrument, typically labeled Z, offers a way around this. Z needs to move the treatment X, but it must do so without having any direct line to the outcome Y except through X. Picture the causal chain as Z pointing to X, and X pointing to Y, while Z stays unrelated to the unobserved factors U that also drive Y.

- Z is correlated with X, giving it something to work with.
- Z has no direct effect on Y once X is accounted for.
- Z does not share common causes with the unobserved determinants of Y.

When those conditions hold, the variation in X that comes from Z behaves like a small, naturally occurring experiment.

## Formal definition and the IV assumptions you must check

The [Columbia Population Health Methods](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/research/population-health-methods/instrumental-variables) summary frames IV as a method for estimating causal relationships in observational data when a trial is not feasible, built on three core conditions:

1. **Relevance (IV1):** Z must be statistically associated with X. This is the one assumption you can test directly, usually with a first-stage regression and its F-statistic.
2. **Independence or exchangeability (IV2):** Z must be as good as randomly assigned with respect to the outcome, meaning it shares no common cause with the unobserved determinants of Y.
3. **Exclusion restriction (IV3):** Z can influence Y only through X, with no direct or back-door pathway.

Relevance is empirical. Independence and exclusion are not, and no data pattern can confirm them on its own: they rest on subject-matter reasoning about how the instrument came to vary. A fourth condition, monotonicity, is usually added so the instrument moves everyone's treatment status in the same direction. Under monotonicity, the [Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin framework](https://math.mcgill.ca/dstephens/AngristIV1996-JASA-Combined.pdf) shows that the IV estimate identifies a Local Average Treatment Effect, not the effect for the whole population. Knowing which assumptions you can check and which you can only argue for shapes how a methods section should be written.

## How two-stage least squares estimates the effect

Two-stage least squares (2SLS) is the workhorse estimator for linear IV models, and it proceeds in two clearly defined steps.

- **Stage one:** regress X on Z and any covariates, then save the predicted values of X. The strength of this stage is judged by the first-stage F-statistic, with values well above the old rule-of-thumb of 10 generally preferred.
- **Stage two:** regress Y on the predicted X (and the same covariates), reading the coefficient on predicted X as the IV estimate of the causal effect.

Because the second stage uses a generated regressor, standard errors need adjustment: most software computes 2SLS standard errors directly rather than running the two regressions by hand, and clustered or heteroskedasticity-robust options should match whatever structure the data has. Common implementations include Stata's `ivreg2`, R's `AER` package, and the `ivreg` command family. When a model has more instruments than endogenous regressors, it is overidentified, and researchers often turn to generalized method of moments (GMM), which can be more efficient and supports the overidentification tests covered later.

## Interpreting IV estimates: LATE, not an average effect

An IV estimate rarely describes everyone in your sample. Under monotonicity, it describes compliers: the people whose treatment status actually shifts because of the instrument, as distinct from always-takers and never-takers whose behavior does not respond to Z. The Columbia summary ties this LATE interpretation directly to the three core IV conditions.

## Weak instruments: diagnosis and practical fixes

A weak instrument barely moves the treatment, and that weakness has consequences beyond a wide confidence interval. The [Harvard survey on weak instruments](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/stock/files/wirev_080218-_posted.pdf) explains that weak instruments pull the 2SLS estimate toward the OLS estimate and distort the standard inference you would normally trust.

**Weak instruments remain a recurring problem in applied IV work**, and the weak-instrument literature notes that first-stage F-statistics in published papers often fall into a range that should prompt caution rather than confidence.

- Check the conventional first-stage F-statistic, then move to the effective F-statistic from Montiel Olea and Pflueger when the data are heteroskedastic or clustered.
- Consider the Kleibergen-Paap statistic in panel or clustered settings as a robustness check on instrument strength.
- When instruments look weak, report an Anderson-Rubin or conditional likelihood ratio confidence set, since these remain valid even when the first stage is shaky.
- If possible, find a stronger instrument or trim a weak one from an overidentified model rather than keeping it for the sake of identification.
- LIML, Fuller's modified estimator, and jackknife 2SLS can reduce bias relative to standard 2SLS in some weak-instrument settings, as the [Annual Reviews guide](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-economics-092123-111021) discusses.

## Validating instruments: falsification and sensitivity checks

Because independence and exclusion cannot be proven outright, a credible IV paper leans on falsification strategies instead of claims alone. The guidance in the [PMC review of IV assumptions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6368955/) recommends pairing subject-matter arguments with empirical checks that could, in principle, reveal a violation.

## A worked example: education, earnings, and quarter of birth

A classic sketch makes the mechanics concrete. Suppose the outcome Y is adult earnings, the treatment X is years of education, and the instrument Z is quarter of birth, which affects how much schooling a person completes before compulsory attendance laws allow them to leave, without plausibly affecting earnings on its own.

- The first stage regresses years of education on quarter-of-birth indicators; a healthy F-statistic here signals that birth timing meaningfully predicts schooling.
- The second stage regresses earnings on the predicted education values from stage one.
- The resulting coefficient is read as the LATE for the compliers whose schooling was actually extended by compulsory attendance rules tied to their birth quarter, not as the return to education for everyone.
- The exclusion restriction deserves its own paragraph in any write-up: does quarter of birth plausibly affect earnings through any channel other than schooling, such as school-starting age effects or relative maturity among classmates?

## What the IV literature gets right, and what gets oversold

The honest judgment here is that IV is a narrow tool, and most of its reputation for solving endogeneity rests on cases where a genuinely strong, well-argued instrument exists. The conventional advice to "just find an instrument" undersells how rare a clean one actually is: a weak or poorly justified instrument can produce an estimate that looks precise and is still wrong, which is arguably worse than an honest OLS regression with disclosed limitations.

The first-stage F-statistic also gets more faith than it deserves. A single number above an old rule-of-thumb does not settle the question, particularly with clustered or heteroskedastic data, where effective F-statistics and identification-robust confidence sets tell a more honest story. If there is one habit worth adopting before any other, it is treating the exclusion restriction as the hypothesis that needs the most scrutiny and the most falsification evidence, not a line to assert and move past. Readers who internalize that compliers, not the full population, are what the estimate describes will write far more defensible methods sections than readers chasing a single clean coefficient.
