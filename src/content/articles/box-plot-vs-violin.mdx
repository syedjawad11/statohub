Use a box plot when you need a compact, standardized summary of several groups, with the median, spread and outliers visible at a glance. Use a violin plot when the shape of each distribution matters, because a violin adds a smoothed density curve that can reveal multiple peaks, gaps and skew that a box hides. With small samples, plot the raw points instead of either one.

The rest of this guide explains why, shows a worked example, and gives a practical decision path so you can choose in under a minute.

## What a box plot actually shows

A box plot (also called a box-and-whiskers plot) condenses a numeric variable into a handful of landmarks. The box spans the first quartile (Q1, the value below which 25% of observations fall) to the third quartile (Q3, the 75% mark), and a line inside marks the median. The distance from Q1 to Q3 is the interquartile range, or IQR, which describes where the middle half of the data sits.

The whiskers extend toward the minimum and maximum, and points beyond them are drawn individually as outliers. In the common convention, a point is flagged when it falls more than 1.5 times the IQR beyond the box, a rule [described in Claus Wilke's visualization text](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/boxplots-violins.html). If you want the arithmetic, our guide to <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-outliers')}>finding outliers with the 1.5×IQR rule</Link> walks through the formula, and <Link to={routes.article('box-plot-interpretation')}>box plot interpretation</Link> covers how to read each element.

The [CDC's data visualization guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/cove/data-visualization-types/box-and-whiskers-plot.html) describes what the format is good at: at a glance you can see how tightly data are grouped, how skewed they are and how symmetric they look. That is a lot of information in very little space, and it explains why the plot is a staple of exploratory work.

### Why box plots became the default

Box plots were introduced by John Tukey in the early 1970s, at a time when graphics were drawn by hand, as [Wilke notes](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/boxplots-violins.html). A five-number summary is easy to draw with a pencil and a ruler. A smooth density curve is not. Much of the box plot's popularity is therefore historical, and that history is a reason to ask whether it is still the best tool when software draws the alternative for free.

## What a violin plot adds

A violin plot is a statistical graphic for comparing probability distributions. As [Wikipedia's entry on the violin plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violin_plot) puts it, it resembles a box plot but adds a rotated kernel density plot on each side. A kernel density estimate (KDE) is a smoothed curve built by placing a small bump on every observation and adding the bumps up. Where observations pile up, the curve is high, so the violin is wide. Where data are sparse, the violin is narrow.

The idea was formalized in an article by Hintze and Nelson in [The American Statistician](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00031305.1998.10480559), which proposed the violin plot as a way to pool the best features of the box plot and the density trace. Their [paper is also available as a PDF](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~rnugent/PCMI2016/papers/ViolinPlots.pdf) if you want the original argument.

The key practical point is that the violin keeps the summary and adds the shape. [Matplotlib's gallery comparison](https://matplotlib.org/stable/gallery/statistics/boxplot_vs_violin.html) makes the same observation: violins are closely related to Tukey's box plots but add the distribution of the sample through the density trace.

## How to read a violin plot

Reading a violin takes four steps. Take them in order and you avoid the usual misreadings.

1. **Find the center.** Look for the median marker, usually a dot or a line, often with a thin box or interval inside the violin. If the plot has no inner marker, you cannot read the median reliably from the outline alone.
2. **Read the width as concentration.** Wider sections mean more observations in that range, and narrower sections mean fewer. The width describes relative density, not a count and not a probability you can read off directly.
3. **Count the peaks.** One bulge suggests one cluster. Two bulges suggest two subgroups mixed together, such as fast and slow responders or two product types. A flat, wide violin suggests values spread fairly evenly.
4. **Check the tails.** A long thin tail on one side signals skew. Our guide to <Link to={routes.article('skewed-distribution')}>skewed distributions</Link> explains how to tell left skew from right skew and what each does to the mean and median.

One more caution belongs here. Because the shape comes from a density estimate, the plot is an aid to understanding, not a tool for detailed mathematical analysis, and you should not mistake relative concentration for actual probability values, as the [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violin_plot) warns.

## A worked example where the box plot misleads

The following numbers are a constructed illustration, not real data. Suppose a support team compares ticket resolution times, in minutes, for two queues, each with 200 tickets.

- **Queue A:** times cluster around 5 to 6 minutes, with a modest right tail.
- **Queue B:** half the tickets are quick password resets that take about 2 minutes, and the other half are billing disputes that take about 9 minutes. Almost nothing falls near 5 or 6 minutes.

Compute the summaries and both queues can report a median near 5.5 minutes and a similar IQR. Their box plots would look almost identical. A manager would conclude the queues perform alike.

Now draw violins. Queue A shows one hump centered near 5.5. Queue B shows two humps with a pinched waist in the middle, where the median sits. The median of Queue B describes almost no actual ticket. The right conclusion is that Queue B is really two processes, and averaging them hides both. This is the situation Wilke describes when he says [violin plots expose differences in density that a box plot can hide](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/boxplots-violins.html), including a normal and a bimodal distribution sharing the same median and IQR.

The lesson is not that box plots are wrong. It is that a box plot answers "where is the middle and how far does it spread?" and stays silent on "how many groups are in here?"

The outlier row matters in practice. Box plots define outliers as points beyond 1.5 times the IQR, whereas violin plots lack that universal rule, a contrast that [Wilke's chapter](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/boxplots-violins.html) draws explicitly. If your audience cares about flagging unusual values, the box plot does that job by design.

## When to use box plots

Choose a box plot when one or more of the following holds.

- **You are comparing many groups.** Twelve monthly boxes fit on one axis and stay readable. Twelve violins often turn into visual noise.
- **The question is about medians, spread or outliers.** The box encodes exactly those quantities. The [StatPearls review of exploratory analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557570/) lists these same uses: examining outliers, assessing range and showing differences among groups.
- **Your audience reads box plots fluently.** In a journal or a regulatory report, a familiar format lowers the risk of misreading.
- **The sample is moderate and the shape is plausibly unimodal.** If a histogram would show one hump, the box plot loses little.
- **You need exact quartiles on the page.** A box can be labeled with them directly.

If you are deciding between a box plot and a histogram rather than a violin, see our comparison of <Link to={routes.article('histogram-vs-boxplot')}>histogram vs box plot</Link> and the guide to <Link to={routes.article('histogram-interpretation')}>histogram interpretation</Link>.

## When a violin plot is the better choice

Reach for a violin when shape could change the decision.

- **You suspect subgroups.** Mixed populations, such as two customer segments or two measurement batches, show up as multiple peaks.
- **Skew is the story.** Wait times, incomes and response times are often right-skewed, and a violin shows the tail's weight, not just its endpoint.
- **You have a moderate number of groups and plenty of data in each.** Three to six violins with a few hundred points each is a comfortable use.
- **You are in exploratory mode.** The violin is a diagnostic. Part of <Link to={routes.article('exploratory-data-analysis')}>an exploratory data analysis workflow</Link> is looking for structure you did not expect, and density shapes help.

## Where both plots go wrong

### Very small samples

Both plots can mislead when there are few observations. A [Nature Methods column on box plots](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.2813) advises avoiding them for very small samples (fewer than 5 observations), because the quartiles are unstable. A smoothed violin built on a handful of points is worse, since it implies a continuous shape that the data cannot support. With samples this small, plot every point, using jitter (a small random nudge along the non-value axis) to stop overlapping dots from hiding each other. Wilke makes the same recommendation: [raw points are more honest than summaries or smoothed shapes at small n](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/boxplots-violins.html).

### Bandwidth and phantom structure

The bandwidth is the smoothing parameter of the KDE. Set it too narrow and the curve becomes jagged, with spurious peaks around individual points. Set it too wide and genuine peaks merge into one. The KDE can also extend past the observed minimum and maximum, suggesting values that never occurred, which is the kind of [phantom data in the tails that Wilke warns about](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/boxplots-violins.html). Many software packages let you trim the violin at the data range and change the bandwidth. Try at least two settings before you trust a second peak.

### Unequal group sizes

A violin from 30 observations and a violin from 3,000 can look equally authoritative. Print the sample size under each group, or scale widths with the count, and say which you did.

### Dropping the outlier information

A violin can bury a single extreme value in a thin tail. If outliers matter, keep a box or mark them, and check them against the <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-outliers')}>1.5×IQR rule</Link>.

## Drawing and labeling the plot well

Whichever you choose, a few habits make the figure honest.

- Label the axis with units and state what the box and whiskers represent, since whisker definitions differ between tools.
- Report the sample size per group.
- Keep the same axis range across panels so groups are comparable.
- Order groups meaningfully, by median or by a natural sequence such as month, not alphabetically by default.
- State the bandwidth method for violins in a caption or note.

These match the broader principles in our <Link to={routes.article('data-visualization-best-practices')}>data visualization best practices guide</Link>. If you are learning to draw statistical graphs by hand for an exam, these [tips for drawing graphs accurately](https://mathvault.io/drawing-graphs-accurately) cover the precision habits that earn marks, and they transfer well to a hand-drawn box plot.

## Our perspective

We see the box plot vs violin plot question as a question about what decision the chart supports, not about which plot is more sophisticated. A violin is not an upgrade on a box plot. It trades simplicity and standardization for shape, and it brings a smoothing choice that the analyst, not the data, controls. That makes it a good diagnostic and a risky default for audiences who cannot see the settings.

Our habit is to look at the shape first, with a histogram or a violin plus raw points, then to decide what to show. If the shape is simple, a box plot communicates it cleanly. If it is not, we show the shape and say so. In both cases we write the sample size on the figure, because it is the cheapest piece of context and the one most often missing.
