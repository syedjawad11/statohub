To find the mean, add up every value in a data set and divide by how many values there are — that single operation, sum divided by count, is the entire calculation. The part that actually trips people up isn't the arithmetic; it's the judgment calls around it: whether a zero belongs in the sum, whether a blank cell should be counted at all, how to handle a frequency table instead of a raw list, and — the mistake that quietly corrupts more reports than any other — whether you can average a set of averages the same way you average raw numbers. You usually can't, and this guide shows exactly why, with numbers you can check yourself.

## What Is the Mean?

The mean is the arithmetic average of a data set: add every value together and divide by how many values there are. It measures the center of the data by spreading the total evenly across every observation — replace every value in the list with the mean, and the sum stays exactly the same, which is the defining property behind the mean formula.

Statisticians write the sample mean as **x̄** (x-bar) and the population mean as **μ** (mu). Both apply the identical calculation — sum divided by count — and only the symbol signals whether the data is a subset of a larger group or the entire group itself (more on that distinction below).

"Mean" and "average" get used interchangeably in casual speech, but they aren't always the same calculation — a geometric or harmonic average answers a different question than the arithmetic one this guide covers. The <Link to={routes.article('what-is-an-average')}>guide to what "average" means</Link> walks through how mean, median, and mode each earn the label "average" in different situations.

## What Is the Mean Formula?

The average formula — also called the mean formula — in plain terms, is:

```
Mean = Sum of all values / Number of values
```

In formal notation, the equation for average is written:

```
x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ... + xₙ) / n
```

Using the summation operator Σ, the same formula for average becomes:

```
x̄ = Σxᵢ / n
```

where **Σxᵢ** is the sum of every individual value and **n** is the total count of values. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm) defines the mean this same way — "the sum of the data points divided by the number of data points" — and treats it as the primary measure of location for a data set, alongside the median and mode.

That one formula covers every notation you'll run into:

Whichever form you reach for, it's the same formula for an average — only the symbol changes across that table; the arithmetic underneath, sum then divide, is identical in every row.

## How Do You Find the Mean, Step by Step?

Finding the mean — or, phrased the way people usually search for it, how to calculate average or how to find average — breaks down into four repeatable steps that work for any list of numbers, large or small.

1. **List every value.** Write out the full data set. Count repeated values separately — three 7s contribute to the sum three times and to the count three times.
2. **Add every value together.** Sum the entire list to get the total. This is the Σxᵢ term in the mean formula, and every data point should go in — don't skip any.
3. **Count the values.** Count the individual data points, not the sum you just produced. This is `n` (or `N` for a population) in the formula above.
4. **Divide the sum by the count.** `Mean = Sum ÷ Count`. The quotient is the mean.

```
Mean = Sum ÷ Count
```

The mean formula never changes; only the data going into it does.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Write down `n =` before you add anything. Deciding the count first forces you to confirm exactly which values belong in the data set, which heads off the most common mistake below: dividing by the wrong number because a value was missed or double-counted.
</Callout>

## What's the Difference Between a Sample Mean and a Population Mean?

A **population mean (μ)** covers every member of a defined group — every student in one specific classroom of 30, for example — and is written `μ = Σxᵢ / N`, where `N` is the size of the entire population. A **sample mean (x̄)** covers only a subset drawn from a larger population — say, 200 of the 10,000 customers in a database — and is written `x̄ = Σxᵢ / n`, where `n` is the size of that sample.

The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's overview of quantitative techniques](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35.htm) draws the same line: "the population, or true, mean is the sum of all the members of the given population divided by the number of members," while "the sample mean is calculated by summing the values in the sample and dividing by the number of values in the sample," with the sample mean then used as a point estimate of the unknown population mean.

Both calculations satisfy the same mean formula — sum divided by count — and the symbol is all that changes. The practical rule: if your data is a sample (almost always the case in applied work), write x̄ and treat it as an estimate, not a guaranteed match for μ. For the fuller statistic-versus-parameter distinction behind that symbol swap, see the <Link to={routes.article('what-is-an-average')}>what-is-an-average guide's section on sample and population means</Link>.

## Why Can't You Average a Set of Averages Directly?

This is the mistake that gets past people who otherwise know exactly how to find the mean: averaging averages together as if they were raw data points. It only gives the right answer when every group behind those averages is the same size — the moment group sizes differ, the naive average is wrong, and wrong in a specific, checkable direction.

Say a delivery company tracks average delivery time by route for one day:

<DataTable
  caption="Delivery time by route, one day"
  headers={['Route', 'Packages delivered', 'Mean delivery time']}
  align={['left', 'right', 'right']}
  rows={[
    ['A', '40', '22 minutes'],
    ['B', '15', '35 minutes'],
    ['C', '25', '18 minutes'],
  ]}
/>

A manager who wants the company-wide average delivery time might just average the three route means:

```
(22 + 35 + 18) / 3 = 75 / 3 = 25 minutes
```

That looks reasonable, and it's wrong — it gives Route B's 15 packages the same influence as Route A's 40. To get the true company-wide mean, go back to actual minutes: multiply each route's mean by its package count to recover the minutes that route spent, sum those totals, then divide by the total packages.

```
Total minutes = (40 × 22) + (15 × 35) + (25 × 18)
             = 880 + 525 + 450
             = 1 855 minutes

Total packages = 40 + 15 + 25 = 80

Correct mean = 1 855 / 80 = 23.1875 ≈ 23.19 minutes
```

<Figure number={1} caption="The naive average of the three route means overstates the true company-wide mean by about 1.8 minutes, because it treats Route B's 15 packages as if they carried the same weight as Route A's 40.">
  <AnnotatedChart
    title="Naive average vs. weighted average of delivery times"
    desc="Bar chart comparing two ways of combining three route averages: the naive average of the three means comes to 25 minutes, while the correct package-weighted average comes to 23.19 minutes."

    points={[
      { label: 'Naive average of the 3 means', value: 25, annotation: 'Ignores package counts' },
      { label: 'Weighted by package count', value: 23.19, annotation: 'The true company-wide mean' },

The naive average of averages gives 25 minutes; the weighted, package-count-correct average gives 23.19 minutes — because the naive figure silently credits Route B, the smallest and slowest route, with a third of the company's volume, when it actually carried less than a fifth of it.

The fix above is just the general weighted-mean formula, applied with each route's package count as its weight:

```
Weighted Mean = Σ(wᵢ × xᵢ) / Σwᵢ
```

The same formula handles any situation where values should count unevenly for a reason other than group size — a final exam worth 60% of a grade and homework worth 40% gives a student who scores 82 on the exam and 90 on homework a weighted mean of 0.60 × 82 + 0.40 × 90 = 85.2, not the simple average of 86 that ignores the weighting.

The rule to remember: you can average a set of averages directly only when the groups behind them are equal in size. The instant group sizes differ, weight each average by its own group size before combining — the <Link to={routes.article('weighted-average')}>weighted average guide</Link> covers the formula in full, with GPA and portfolio-weighting examples.

## How Do You Handle Zeros, Missing Values, and Frequency Tables When Finding the Mean?

Three situations account for most of the real-world mean calculations that go wrong even when the sum-and-divide step is done correctly.

### Zeros vs. missing values

A zero and a missing value are not the same thing, and confusing them changes the answer. A zero is a real, recorded measurement of "none" and belongs in both the sum and the count. A blank or missing entry means no measurement was taken, and belongs in neither.

Take a rain gauge logging daily rainfall (mm) over one week: Monday 0, Tuesday 0, Wednesday 12, Thursday *(sensor offline — no reading)*, Friday 5, Saturday 0, Sunday 0. Handled correctly, the offline day is excluded from both the sum and the count, leaving six actual readings:

```
Sum = 0 + 0 + 12 + 5 + 0 + 0 = 17
Count = 6 (Thursday is not counted)
Mean = 17 / 6 ≈ 2.83 mm/day
```

Handled incorrectly — treating the missing Thursday reading as a rainfall of 0 mm — the count becomes 7 instead of 6:

```
Sum = 17 (unchanged)
Count = 7 (Thursday miscounted as a zero)
Mean = 17 / 7 ≈ 2.43 mm/day
```

<Callout variant="note" title="Which way the error points">
Treating a missing value as a zero always pushes the mean too low, never too high — a phantom zero gets added to the count without adding anything to the sum. Before summing anything, decide which blanks are genuine zeros (keep them) and which are missing data (drop them from the count too).
</Callout>

### Frequency tables

When data arrives as a frequency table — each value listed with how many times it occurs, the same basic layout [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-2-histograms-frequency-polygons-and-time-series-graphs) shows paired with class boundaries and running totals — multiply each value by its frequency, sum those products, then divide by the total count:

```
Mean = Σ(xᵢ × fᵢ) / Σfᵢ
```

This is mathematically equivalent to listing every individual value and applying the standard formula; the frequency table just compresses repeated entries. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, 2.5 Measures of the Center of the Data](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-5-measures-of-the-center-of-the-data) makes the equivalence explicit: when a value repeats, "the mean can be calculated by multiplying each distinct value by its frequency and then dividing the sum by the total number of data values."

### A single outlier

A single extreme value can pull the mean far from where most of the data sits. If nine factory workers earn $42,000 a year and the CEO earns $900,000, the mean salary is:

```
(9 × 42 000 + 900 000) / 10 = 1 278 000 / 10 = $127 800
```

That $127,800 figure misrepresents what a typical worker earns, even though the arithmetic is correct. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's guidance on outliers](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc16.htm) treats exactly this kind of extreme value as worth flagging before it's allowed to drive a summary statistic unchallenged; the <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-outliers')}>outlier detection guide</Link> covers the standard 1.5×IQR method for finding one. The mean formula is still valid here — it just tells a story the median usually tells more honestly.

## How Do You Find the Mean in Excel, Google Sheets, or Python?

Every major tool implements the mean formula as a single built-in function, but each handles blanks and zeros slightly differently.

```
Excel / Google Sheets:  =AVERAGE(range)

Python:  values = [143, 168, 155, 201, 179]
         import statistics
         statistics.mean(values)   # 169.2
```

In Excel, [Microsoft's documentation for the `AVERAGE` function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/average-function-047bac88-d466-426c-a32b-8f33eb960cf6) is explicit about this: "If a range or cell reference argument contains text, logical values, or empty cells, those values are ignored; however, cells with the value zero are included." That's exactly the distinction covered above — a truly blank cell drops out of both the sum and the count automatically, but a typed `0` stays in as a real zero. Google Sheets' [`AVERAGE` function](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/3093615) uses the same core definition — the sum of the value arguments divided by how many of them there are — and also ignores any text it encounters, though its own documentation doesn't spell out empty-cell handling the way Microsoft's does. Store missing readings as blank cells rather than zeros, and Excel's `AVERAGE` gets it right without any extra handling.

In Python, the [`statistics` module's documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/statistics.html) describes `statistics.mean()` as returning "the sample arithmetic mean of data," which is a plain, unweighted sum-divided-by-count over whatever values you pass in — it does not skip missing entries, so filter a list before calling it if some readings are genuinely absent (represented as `None` or excluded from the list) rather than real zeros.

## A Worked Example: Overnight Server Response Times

A site reliability engineer pulls five overnight API response times, in milliseconds, to check whether a service is meeting its latency target: **143, 168, 155, 201, 179**. The goal — a real-world case of how to find the average of numbers pulled straight from a monitoring dashboard — is to find the mean response time.

1. **List the values.** The five readings are 143, 168, 155, 201, and 179 ms.
2. **Add them together.**

   ```
   143 + 168 + 155 + 201 + 179 = 846
   ```

3. **Count the values.** There are **5** readings.
4. **Divide.**

   ```
   Mean = 846 / 5 = 169.2
   ```

The mean response time is **169.2 ms**. That tells the engineer that, averaged across the overnight window, requests came back in about 169.2 ms — useful for comparing this night against last night's mean, or against a 200 ms service-level target. No single request took exactly 169.2 ms, and that's expected: the mean represents what every request would have logged if the total milliseconds were shared equally across all five — 5 × 169.2 = 846, exactly the original sum.

A quick way to verify any mean: the result must lie between the minimum and maximum values in the data set. Here the readings range from 143 ms to 201 ms, so 169.2 ms falls squarely inside that range. A mean outside the range of the original data always signals an arithmetic error — retrace the addition step first.

## Common Mistakes When Finding the Mean

- **Counting the sum instead of the values.** Count the number of data points you summed, not the total itself. If 8 values add to 48, divide 48 by 8 — not by 48 or 1.
- **Accidentally including outliers or typos.** Entering 880 instead of 88 can radically distort the mean. Scan the list for implausibly large or small values before calculating, and verify anything that looks wrong against the original source.
- **Dividing by the wrong count when values are missing.** If a data set has 10 slots but one value was never recorded, dividing by 10 instead of 9 gives a mean that's too low — the same error covered above under zeros versus missing values.
- **Rounding intermediate results.** Rounding the sum before dividing compounds error into the final answer. Carry full precision until the last step, then round the mean itself.
- **Averaging averages without weighting.** Combining group averages by taking their plain average, instead of weighting each by group size, is one of the most common — and hardest to spot — real-world mean errors, as the delivery-route example above shows directly.

## Statohub's Take on Finding the Mean

Statohub's position is that the arithmetic of finding the mean is the easy part; the judgment calls around it are where accuracy is actually won or lost. A mean computed from the wrong count, or from averaged averages that were never weighted, is still "a mean" — it's just the wrong number, confidently reported. Decide what belongs in your sum and your count before you touch a calculator, and the four-step process handles the rest reliably every time.
