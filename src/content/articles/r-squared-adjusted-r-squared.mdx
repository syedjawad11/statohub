**R squared** (R², also called the coefficient of determination) measures how well a regression line fits a dataset — the share of the variance in the outcome variable that the model accounts for. An R² of 0.73 means the model explains 73% of the variation in the response; the other 27% comes from factors the model does not capture or from plain randomness. R² ranges from 0 (the model explains nothing) to 1 (it explains everything), and it comes with a close cousin, adjusted R², that corrects for how many predictors went into the model. This guide works through both, with the arithmetic shown at every step.

## What Does R-Squared Measure?

R² quantifies how much of the total variation in a dependent variable y is explained by a regression model, compared with a baseline that just predicts the sample mean ȳ for every observation. That comparison is built from three sums of squares.

The **total sum of squares (SST)** is the spread of the data around the mean-only baseline:

```
SST = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²
```

The fitted model produces a predicted value ŷᵢ for each observation. The **regression sum of squares (SSR)** is the part of that spread the model actually explains:

```
SSR = Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)²
```

The **residual sum of squares (SSE)** is what is left over — the scatter of the actual values around the fitted line:

```
SSE = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
```

    ['SST (total)', 'Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²', 'Total variation in y around its mean'],
    ['SSR (regression)', 'Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)²', 'Variation the model explains'],

/>

These three satisfy the identity SST = SSR + SSE, and R² is simply the explained share of the total. [R's documentation for `summary.lm`](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/summary.lm.html) states this same identity in its own notation, R² = 1 − ΣRᵢ²/Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)², where Rᵢ is the residual for observation i — the same quantity as SSE and SST here, just written with sums instead of names:

```
R² = SSR / SST = 1 − (SSE / SST)
```

An R² of 0.73 means the model explains 73% of the total variance in y; the remaining 27% is residual variation the predictor (or predictors) do not capture.

A quick sanity check worth doing on any dataset before trusting the number: SST, SSR, and SSE should always satisfy SST = SSR + SSE exactly (within rounding). If they don't, the fitted values or the mean were computed incorrectly somewhere upstream — check that before reporting R² at all.

## What Is the R-Squared Formula?

For **simple linear regression** — one predictor, fitted by the method the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's linear least squares page](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section1/pmd141.htm) describes — R² has a direct link to the Pearson correlation coefficient r between x and y:

```
R² = r²
```

That identity is literally where the name "R-squared" comes from: in the one-predictor case, it is the squared Pearson correlation. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/12-3-the-regression-equation) calls r² exactly that — "the coefficient of determination" — when it introduces the regression equation. Squaring r removes its sign, so R² is always non-negative regardless of whether the relationship runs positive or negative. The <Link to={routes.article('correlation-coefficient')}>correlation coefficient guide</Link> covers how r itself is computed and interpreted.

For **multiple regression**, with more than one predictor, that shortcut no longer applies — compute R² directly from the sums of squares instead:

```
R² = 1 − (SSE / SST)
```

This general form works for any number of predictors, while `R² = r²` is only valid with exactly one. For more than one predictor, R² is still interpreted the same way — the share of variance explained — even though it no longer reduces to a single correlation you can square by hand.

## How Do You Interpret an R-Squared Value?

There is no universal "good" R² — the right benchmark depends on the field and what you are trying to predict.

  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[

    ['Social sciences & economics', 'Usually moderate', 'Human behavior carries far more unmeasured variation.'],

  ]}
/>

Three traps come up repeatedly once you move past the headline number:

**Adding predictors inflates R² mechanically.** Least-squares regression can only keep R² the same or raise it when you add a predictor, even pure noise, because the optimizer gains one more free parameter to reduce SSE. That mechanical inflation is exactly why adjusted R² (below) exists.

**A high R² does not guarantee a well-fitting model.** The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's guidance on model validation](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section4/pmd44.htm) is direct about this: "a high R² value does not guarantee that the model fits the data well," and quoting R² alone — without graphical residual analysis — is one of the most common and most overlooked shortcuts in model validation. A model can also show R² near 1 on the data used to fit it and still perform poorly on a held-out sample, a classic sign of overfitting. Always check the residual plot and performance on new or cross-validated data before trusting the fit.

**A high R² is not evidence of causation.** It tells you the model's predicted values track the observed ones closely; it says nothing about whether the predictor causes the outcome. See <Link to={routes.article('correlation-vs-causation')}>correlation vs causation</Link> for the full argument, and <Link to={routes.article('what-is-correlation')}>what is correlation</Link> for how association differs from a causal claim.

**Restricting the range of x can shrink R² even when the underlying relationship is strong.** If you fit a regression only on a narrow slice of x values — say, students who all scored within a few points of each other on an entry exam — the predictor has less room to explain variation in y, and R² drops even though the true relationship between the full ranges of x and y may be just as tight. This is the same range-restriction effect that weakens a correlation coefficient, since R² = r² in the one-predictor case.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Before trusting any R² value, plot the residuals. A model with R² = 0.95 but a clear curve in the residual plot is misspecified — the high R² reflects an accidental alignment with the training points, not a correct functional form.
</Callout>

## A Worked Example: Computing R-Squared by Hand

**Data:** x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and y = 2, 4, 5, 4, 6. These are the linear regression calculator's defaults, so you can check every number below against the tool.

      { label: 'Compute SSE and SST', detail: 'SSE = 2.40, SST = 8.80' },

    ]}

**Step 1 — compute the means.**

```
x̄ = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5) / 5 = 3
ȳ = (2 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 6) / 5 = 4.2
```

**Step 2 — fit the least-squares line.** The slope and intercept come from the same cross-products used for the correlation coefficient. Work across each point:

| xᵢ | yᵢ | xᵢ − x̄ | yᵢ − ȳ | (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) | (xᵢ − x̄)² |
|----|----|---------|---------|--------------------|------------|
| 1  | 2  | −2      | −2.2    | 4.4                | 4          |
| 2  | 4  | −1      | −0.2    | 0.2                | 1          |
| 3  | 5  | 0       | 0.8     | 0                  | 0          |
| 4  | 4  | 1       | −0.2    | −0.2               | 1          |
| 5  | 6  | 2       | 1.8     | 3.6                | 4          |
| **Sum** | | |         | **8.0**            | **10**     |

```
Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = 8.0
Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²        = 10.0

b₁ = 8.0 / 10.0 = 0.8
b₀ = ȳ − b₁ · x̄ = 4.2 − 0.8 × 3 = 1.8
```

Fitted line: **ŷ = 1.8 + 0.8x**

**Step 3 — compute predicted values, SSE, and SST.**

| xᵢ | yᵢ | ŷᵢ | (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² | (yᵢ − ȳ)² |
|----|----|----|------------|-----------|
| 1  | 2  | 2.6 | 0.36      | 4.84      |
| 2  | 4  | 3.4 | 0.36      | 0.04      |
| 3  | 5  | 4.2 | 0.64      | 0.64      |
| 4  | 4  | 5.0 | 1.00      | 0.04      |
| 5  | 6  | 5.8 | 0.04      | 3.24      |
| **Sum** | | | **2.40 (SSE)** | **8.80 (SST)** |

**Step 4 — compute R².** Two equivalent routes, both using the SSE and SST from Step 3:

```
SSR = SST − SSE = 8.80 − 2.40 = 6.40

R² = SSR / SST       = 6.40 / 8.80 ≈ 0.727
R² = 1 − (SSE / SST) = 1 − (2.40 / 8.80) ≈ 0.727
```

**Interpretation:** the regression line explains about 72.7% of the variance in y; the remaining 27.3% is residual scatter the line does not capture. You can confirm this through the correlation route too: `r = 8.0 / √(10.0 × 8.80) ≈ 0.8528`, and `r² ≈ 0.7273` — the same result, as the formula in the previous section predicts.

## What Is Adjusted R-Squared?

**Adjusted R²** corrects the ordinary coefficient of determination for the number of predictors in the model. Because adding any predictor — even a meaningless one — can only hold R² steady or push it up, raw R² inflates as a model grows. Adjusted R² penalizes that inflation by dividing by residual degrees of freedom instead of total degrees of freedom, a correction documented alongside the ordinary coefficient in [OpenIntro's *Introduction to Modern Statistics*, multiple-regression chapter](https://openintro-ims.netlify.app/model-mlr.html):

```
Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²) × (n − 1) / (n − k − 1)
```

where n is the number of observations and k is the number of predictors (not counting the intercept). The multiplier `(n − 1) / (n − k − 1)` is always ≥ 1, so adjusted R² is always ≤ R². Adjusted R² rises when a new predictor genuinely helps and falls when a predictor contributes less than chance would predict.

**Continuing the worked example** with n = 5, k = 1, R² ≈ 0.7273:

```
Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − 0.7273) × (5 − 1) / (5 − 1 − 1)
            = 1 − 0.2727 × (4 / 3)
            = 1 − 0.3636
            = 0.6364
```

The gap between R² (0.727) and adjusted R² (0.636) is large here mainly because n = 5 is tiny; with n = 100 and the same R² and k, adjusted R² works out to about 0.7245 — nearly identical to R².

**What happens if you add a near-useless second predictor to this same dataset?** Suppose it nudges R² up only slightly, to 0.735, with n still 5 but k now 2:

```
Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − 0.735) × (5 − 1) / (5 − 2 − 1)
            = 1 − 0.265 × (4 / 2)
            = 1 − 0.53
            = 0.47
```

R² rose from 0.727 to 0.735, but adjusted R² fell from 0.636 to 0.47 — the predictor cost more in lost degrees of freedom than it added in explained variance. That divergence is the entire point of the statistic.

<Callout variant="note" title="Adjusted R² can go negative">
With enough predictors relative to observations, adjusted R² can drop below zero. For n = 20 and k = 15 predictors with R² = 0.20, adjusted R² works out to 1 − (1 − 0.20) × 19/4 = −2.80 — a clear signal the model is badly overfit relative to its sample size.
</Callout>

## When Should You Use Adjusted R-Squared Instead of R-Squared?

  ]}
/>

In simple regression with a reasonably large sample, R² and adjusted R² sit close together because there is only one predictor to penalize and the `(n − 1)/(n − k − 1)` multiplier is close to 1. With a small sample, that gap can still be large — the worked example above has only n = 5, which is exactly why its R² (0.727) and adjusted R² (0.636) are noticeably different despite having just one predictor. The gap widens further as you add predictors in multiple regression, which is exactly when the correction starts to matter for deciding whether a variable earns its place in the model.

Adjusted R² is a good first check for model selection, but it is not the only one. Many analysts also look at the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which apply different complexity penalties and can behave differently when a model is badly misspecified. Treat adjusted R² as a quick, intuitive screen and confirm a close call with one of those criteria before finalizing a model.

## What Are Real-World Applications of R-Squared?

R² shows up anywhere a model needs to be judged against raw data, not just in textbook regression exercises:

- **Finance.** CAPM and factor-model regressions report R² to show how much of a stock's or portfolio's return variation is explained by market movements or named risk factors, with the unexplained share attributed to idiosyncratic risk.
- **Economics.** Macroeconomic models that regress consumption, GDP growth, or inflation on a handful of predictors use R² as a first-pass check of explanatory power before moving to more detailed diagnostics.
- **Calibration curves.** Laboratory instruments are calibrated by regressing a known concentration or quantity against an instrument reading; a near-1 R² on that calibration line is often required before the instrument is trusted for unknown samples.
- **Machine learning baselines.** Before adopting a complex model, practitioners often fit a simple linear regression and report its R² as a baseline that the complex model needs to beat by a meaningful margin, not just a fractional amount.
- **Growth and dose-response curves.** Biology and agriculture use R² to judge how well a fitted growth curve or dose-response model tracks observed measurements across experimental conditions.

Across all of these, R² answers the same narrow question — how much of the observed variation does this specific model explain — and none of them treat it as proof that the model is correctly specified or causal.

## How Do You Calculate R-Squared in Excel, Python, and R?

```
Excel:   =RSQ(known_y's, known_x's)         simple-regression R² directly
         LINEST() with stats=TRUE            full regression output incl. R²

Python:  from sklearn.metrics import r2_score
         r2_score(y_true, y_pred)             R² from actual vs. predicted values

         import statsmodels.api as sm
         model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
         model.rsquared                       R²
         model.rsquared_adj                   adjusted R², computed directly

R:       model <- lm(y ~ x)
         summary(model)$r.squared             R²
         summary(model)$adj.r.squared         adjusted R²
```

[Microsoft's documentation for the `RSQ` function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/rsq-function-d7161715-250d-4a01-b80d-a8364f2be08f) confirms it returns the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient for paired x and y ranges — the same `R² = r²` identity covered above. [scikit-learn's `r2_score` reference](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.r2_score.html) computes R² from any set of true and predicted values, which also makes it usable for models beyond simple linear regression, though it has no built-in adjusted-R² option. [statsmodels' `RegressionResults` reference](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.regression.linear_model.RegressionResults.html) documents the `rsquared` and `rsquared_adj` attributes that an OLS fit returns directly. [R's documentation for `summary.lm`](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/summary.lm.html) shows that fitting a model with `lm()` and calling `summary()` on it reports both the "Multiple R-squared" and the "Adjusted R-squared" in the same output.

## Common Mistakes With R-Squared

- **Treating R² alone as proof of a good model.** A high R² means the model fits the training data closely; it says nothing about whether the model is correctly specified, practically meaningful, or accurate on data it hasn't seen. Always check residual plots alongside the number, not instead of it.
- **Comparing R² across models with different numbers of predictors.** Raw R² only ever rises as you add variables, including ones that are pure noise, so a "better" R² on a larger model can be meaningless. Switch to adjusted R² — or AIC/BIC — for that comparison.
- **Trusting R² from a very small sample.** With n = 5, as in the worked example, R² is highly sensitive to individual points — remove or change one observation and the value can shift sharply. Treat small-sample R² as a rough estimate, not a precise measurement, and corroborate it with a larger sample before relying on it.
- **Confusing R² with prediction error.** R² is a relative measure of fit, not an absolute one. Two models with the same R² can have very different errors in the original units if their outcome variables are on different scales; compare root-mean-square error (RMSE) or mean absolute error when the original units matter.
- **Assuming linearity.** R² is built from a linear decomposition of variance. Fitting a straight line to a curved relationship understates how predictable the outcome actually is — inspect a scatter plot before trusting a linear R², and consider a polynomial or transformed predictor if the pattern curves.
- **Reading R² as causation.** An R² of 0.85 means the linear model explains 85% of the variance in this particular sample, nothing more. Causation needs a controlled experiment or careful adjustment for confounders, not a high R² on observational data.

## Statohub's Take on R-Squared

Statohub's position is that R² is a useful, honest summary of fit as long as you treat it as relative, not absolute. The single habit worth building is checking whether you are comparing models with the same number of predictors before trusting raw R² at all — if you aren't, adjusted R² is the number that actually answers the question you're asking.
