The **probability formula** calculates how likely an event is to happen, and in its simplest form it equals the number of favorable outcomes divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes. That basic ratio is the starting point for a family of related formulas: the conditional probability formula adjusts for information you already know, the binomial probability formula counts successes across repeated trials, and the formula for expected value — also called the expectation probability formula — gives the long-run average of a random variable. Every probability value falls between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain), whether you express it as a fraction, decimal, or percentage. This guide shows how to find probability with each formula, computing every number by hand so you can check your own work the same way.

## What Is the Probability Formula?

The basic probability formula applies whenever every outcome in an experiment is equally likely. For an event A:

```
P(A) = (number of favorable outcomes) / (total number of possible outcomes)
```

Three pieces make up this probability formula: **P(A)** is the probability of event A, a number between 0 and 1; **favorable outcomes** are the outcomes that count as A happening; and **total outcomes** is every possible result of the experiment. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, section 3.1 on terminology](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/3-1-terminology), defines the sample space as the full list of these possible outcomes — the denominator of every classical probability calculation.

**Example — rolling an even number.** Roll a standard six-sided die. The favorable outcomes are 2, 4, and 6 (three outcomes), out of six total faces:

```
P(even) = 3 / 6 = 0.5
```

**Example — drawing a king.** From a standard 52-card deck, the favorable outcomes are the four kings, out of 52 total cards:

```
P(king) = 4 / 52 = 1 / 13 ≈ 0.0769
```

About a 7.7% chance. Every event also has a complement — the probability that it does *not* occur, written P(A'):

```
P(A') = 1 − P(A)
```

From the card example: P(not a king) = 1 − 4/52 = 48/52 = 12/13 ≈ 0.923. The complement rule holds because every probability in a sample space sums to 1, so it's often faster to count what you *don't* want than what you do.

## How Do You Find Probability When Two Events Combine?

Most real questions involve more than one event. Two rules — addition and multiplication — extend the basic probability formula to handle "or" and "and" questions, and [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, section 3.3 on the two basic rules of probability](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/3-3-two-basic-rules-of-probability), is the standard reference for both.

**Addition rule, mutually exclusive events.** When A and B cannot both happen on the same trial:

```
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)
```

Rolling a 1 or a 6 on one die: P(1 or 6) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333.

**Addition rule, overlapping events.** When A and B can both happen, subtract the overlap so it isn't counted twice:

```
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)
```

Drawing a card that's a spade *or* a face card (jack, queen, king): P(spade) = 13/52, P(face card) = 12/52, and P(spade face card) = 3/52 (the jack, queen, and king of spades). So P(spade or face card) = 13/52 + 12/52 − 3/52 = 22/52 = 11/26 ≈ 0.4231.

**Multiplication rule, independent events.** When one outcome doesn't change the other:

```
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
```

Two coin flips, both tails: P(TT) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 0.25.

**Multiplication rule, dependent events.** When the first outcome changes the odds on the second, the conditional probability of B given A — covered next — replaces the plain P(B):

```
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
```

A bag holds 4 red marbles and 6 blue marbles (10 total). Draw two without replacement. P(first red) = 4/10; given a red came out first, P(second red | first red) = 3/9. So P(both red) = 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 ≈ 0.1333.

## What Is the Conditional Probability Formula?

The **conditional probability formula** gives the probability that event B occurs given that event A has already occurred, written P(B | A) and read "the probability of B given A":

```
P(B | A) = P(A and B) / P(A)
```

This restricts the sample space to outcomes where A is true, then divides by P(A) to re-normalize the remaining probabilities back to a scale that sums to 1.

**Worked example.** A survey of 200 students finds that 120 study math, 80 study physics, and 50 study both subjects. What's the probability a student studies physics, given that they study math?

```
P(math) = 120 / 200 = 0.60
P(math and physics) = 50 / 200 = 0.25

P(physics | math) = 0.25 / 0.60 ≈ 0.4167
```

About 41.7% of math students also study physics — notice this is different from the overall 40% of all 200 students who study physics (80/200), because conditioning on "studies math" changes the relevant population.

Two events are **independent** exactly when P(B | A) = P(B); if that equality fails, the events are dependent. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, section 3.2](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/3-2-independent-and-mutually-exclusive-events), covers that independence test in full, alongside how it differs from mutual exclusivity.

Bayes' theorem reverses the direction of this formula — given that B occurred, it recovers the probability of A from the same three quantities:

```
P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B)
```

P(A) is the prior probability of A before seeing any evidence, P(B | A) is how likely B is if A is true, and P(A | B) is the updated (posterior) probability of A once B is known. The <Link to={routes.article('bayes-theorem')}>Bayes' theorem guide</Link> walks through this formula in full, with worked examples from medical testing and spam filtering.

## What Is the Binomial Probability Formula?

The **binomial probability formula** gives the probability of exactly k successes across n independent trials, each with the same success probability p:

```
P(X = k) = C(n, k) × pᵏ × (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
```

`C(n, k) = n! / (k! × (n − k)!)` is the binomial coefficient, counting how many ways k successes can be arranged among n trials. [NIST/SEMATECH's e-Handbook entry on the binomial distribution](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm) calls it "probably the most commonly used discrete distribution," and [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, section 4.3](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/4-3-binomial-distribution), gives the same formula. This guide checks it against four conditions — a fixed n, two outcomes per trial, constant p, and independent trials — while OpenStax itself groups the same requirements into three characteristics of a binomial experiment, folding constant p and independence into one: "the n trials are independent and are repeated using identical conditions." Either count describes the same model.

**Worked example.** A 5-question multiple-choice quiz has 4 options per question, and a student guesses every answer randomly (p = 0.25 per question). What's the probability of exactly 2 correct guesses?

```
C(5, 2) = 5! / (2! × 3!) = 10
P(X = 2) = 10 × (0.25)² × (0.75)³
         = 10 × 0.0625 × 0.421875
         ≈ 0.2637
```

About a 26.4% chance of landing exactly 2 correct answers by pure guessing.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
"Exactly 2" and "at least 2" are different questions with different formulas — the second needs a sum of several exact-k terms, not a single one. The <Link to={routes.article('binomial-distribution')}>binomial distribution guide</Link> covers the cumulative versions of this formula, the four setup conditions in detail, and how the geometric distribution answers a related but different question.
</Callout>

## What Is the Formula for Expected Value?

The **formula for expected value** — also called the expectation probability formula — gives the long-run average of a random variable. For a discrete random variable X with values x₁, x₂, …, xₙ and probabilities p₁, p₂, …, pₙ:

```
E(X) = x₁ · p₁ + x₂ · p₂ + … + xₙ · pₙ = Σ xᵢ · pᵢ
```

[OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*, section 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/4-2-mean-or-expected-value-and-standard-deviation), defines expected value exactly this way: each outcome weighted by its own probability, then summed.

**Worked example — a raffle ticket.** A raffle sells 1,000 tickets at $5 each, with one $2,000 prize. Buying one ticket gives a 1-in-1,000 chance of winning $2,000 and a 999-in-1,000 chance of winning nothing, minus the $5 cost either way:

```
E(net gain) = (1/1,000) × $2,000 + (999/1,000) × $0 − $5
            = $2 − $5
            = −$3
```

The expected value of one ticket is **−$3**. That doesn't mean you lose exactly $3 — you either win $1,995 net or lose $5 — but across many tickets, the average result converges to a $3 loss per ticket.

When X follows a binomial distribution with n trials and success probability p, expected value simplifies to a closed form:

```
E(X) = n × p
```

For the 5-question guessing quiz above: E(correct guesses) = 5 × 0.25 = **1.25** — on average, a student who guesses randomly on every question gets just over one question right.

<Callout variant="note" title="Watch the interpretation">
Expected value describes an average over many repetitions, not a forecast for one trial. You will never actually score "1.25 correct answers" on a single 5-question quiz — you'll score 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5.
</Callout>

For a continuous random variable, the same idea replaces the sum with an integral over a probability density function (PDF); a single exact value has probability 0, so you instead compute the area under the PDF over an interval. The normal distribution and its <Link to={routes.article('z-table')}>z-table</Link> are the most common tool for that lookup, and the <Link to={routes.article('probability-distribution')}>probability distribution guide</Link> covers the continuous case in full alongside the discrete one above.

## How Do You Calculate Probability Step by Step?

Regardless of which formula applies, the same process gets you to a correct answer:

1. **Define the experiment.** What's the random process — rolling dice, drawing cards, running a trial?
2. **Identify the sample space.** List or count every possible outcome, and confirm whether outcomes are equally likely.
3. **Define the event.** Which outcomes count as "success" for what you're measuring?
4. **Choose the formula.** Basic for equally likely outcomes; conditional when something is already known; binomial for fixed, independent, two-outcome trials; expected value for a long-run average.
5. **Calculate and interpret.** Express the result as a fraction, decimal, or percentage, and confirm the full sample space still sums to 1.
6. **Sanity-check.** A result above 1 or below 0 signals an arithmetic error; a result extremely close to 0 or 1 deserves a second look against the problem description.

## A Worked Example: Will at Least 2 of 6 Recipients Open the Email?

**Setup.** A marketing email has a historical open rate of 20% (p = 0.2), and it's sent to 6 recipients independently (n = 6). What's the probability that at least 2 of them open it?

Directly summing P(X = 2) through P(X = 6) works, but it's faster to use the complement: P(X ≥ 2) = 1 − P(X ≤ 1) = 1 − [P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)].

1. **Compute P(X = 0):** every recipient fails to open.

```
P(X = 0) = C(6, 0) × (0.2)⁰ × (0.8)⁶ = (0.8)⁶ ≈ 0.2621
```

2. **Compute P(X = 1):** exactly one recipient opens.

```
P(X = 1) = C(6, 1) × (0.2)¹ × (0.8)⁵ = 6 × 0.2 × 0.32768 ≈ 0.3932
```

3. **Sum and subtract from 1.**

```
P(X ≤ 1) = 0.2621 + 0.3932 = 0.6554
P(X ≥ 2) = 1 − 0.6554 = 0.3446
```

**Decision.** There's roughly a **34.5% probability** that at least 2 of the 6 recipients open this email. The mean number of opens is μ = 6 × 0.2 = 1.2, with σ = √(6 × 0.2 × 0.8) ≈ 0.98, so getting 2 or more opens means landing at least slightly above the typical outcome — useful context before judging whether a campaign under- or over-performed its historical rate.

## How Do You Calculate Probability in Excel, Python, and R?

```
Excel:   =COMBIN(n, k)                   counts the arrangements, C(n, k)
         =BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, FALSE)     probability of exactly k successes
         =BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, TRUE)      probability of k or fewer successes

Python:  from math import comb
         comb(n, k)                       counts the arrangements, C(n, k)
         from scipy.stats import binom
         binom.pmf(k, n, p)                probability of exactly k successes
         binom.cdf(k, n, p)                 probability of k or fewer successes

R:       choose(n, k)                     counts the arrangements, C(n, k)
         dbinom(k, size = n, prob = p)     probability of exactly k successes
         pbinom(k, size = n, prob = p)     probability of k or fewer successes
```

[Python's `math.comb` documentation](https://docs.python.org/3/library/math.html#math.comb) covers the combinatorics function used for C(n, k), and [Microsoft's reference for `BINOM.DIST`](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/binom-dist-function-c5ae37b6-f39c-4be2-94c2-509a1480770c), [SciPy's `scipy.stats.binom` reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.binom.html), and [R's documentation for the binomial distribution functions](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/Binomial.html) each document their own `pmf`/`cdf`-style arguments for the exact-versus-cumulative switch shown above.

## Common Mistakes When Using Probability Formulas

- **Adding probabilities without checking for overlap.** P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) only holds for mutually exclusive events; overlapping events need the subtraction term, or the total can exceed 1.
- **Multiplying probabilities without checking independence.** P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) only holds for independent events; dependent events need the conditional probability formula instead.
- **Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A).** The probability a test is positive given disease is not the same as the probability of disease given a positive test — reversing conditioning direction needs Bayes' theorem, not a simple swap.
- **Applying the binomial formula to non-independent trials.** Drawing without replacement changes p after each draw, which breaks the binomial model; that scenario calls for the hypergeometric distribution instead.
- **Treating expected value as a single-trial prediction.** E(X) describes a long-run average across many repetitions. On any one trial, the actual outcome is almost never equal to E(X) itself.
- **Mixing up "exactly," "at most," and "at least."** Each phrasing points to a different formula — one exact term versus a sum of several — and conflating them is one of the most common probability errors overall.

## Statohub's Take on the Probability Formula

Statohub's position is that nearly every probability mistake traces back to skipping a verification step, not to faulty arithmetic: checking whether two events can overlap before adding, checking independence before multiplying, and rereading the question's exact wording before picking "exactly," "at most," or "at least." Learn those few checks once, and the five formulas in this guide cover the overwhelming majority of probability questions you'll ever need to answer by hand.
