What is the average? It is a single number chosen to stand in for an entire set of values — but that definition of average hides three different calculations, and picking the wrong one is how honest data ends up telling a misleading story. Say a commute takes 27 minutes on average and most people assume the arithmetic mean did the work. Say the average house on a street sold for $410,000, and that figure could just as easily be a median, chosen precisely because one $2 million teardown would wreck a straight mean. Both sentences use the word "average"; only one of them, most likely, used the mean. This page works through the average meaning, the three calculations that can each answer what is average for identical data, and the handful of cases — skew, weighting, rates, compounding — where the word quietly stops meaning the mean at all.

## What Does "Average" Actually Mean?

Average is the umbrella term for **measures of central tendency** — any single value meant to summarize where a dataset centers. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, 1.3.5.1 — Measures of Location](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm) frames the underlying task as finding "a typical or central value that best describes the data," and lists exactly three common definitions for it: the mean, the median, and the mode.

In everyday language, average meaning blurs together with words like "typical" or "usual." Saying you spend 45 minutes at the gym on average doesn't promise exactly 45 minutes every visit — it describes a general pattern, not a guarantee for any single case. That casual sense is fine for conversation, but statistics needs more precision than that.

To define average precisely, then, you have to name which of the three you mean, because the word by itself doesn't specify a formula — it specifies a job: take many numbers and hand back one that represents them. That's the reason textbooks give more than one average definition instead of settling on a single one, and it's the subject of the next section.

## Is Mean the Same as Average?

Usually, yes: for add-and-divide — sum the values, divide by the count — "mean" and "average" name the same number. That single answer covers every way the question gets asked: is average and mean the same, is average the same as mean, is mean average, is mean same as average, is mean the same as average, is the mean the average, is the mean the same as average, and is the mean the same as the average are really one question in eight different wordings, and mean is the same as average in each of them.

NIST's own handbook treats it exactly this way:

<Callout variant="quote" source="NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, 1.3.5.1" sourceHref="https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm">
The mean is that value that is most commonly referred to as the average. We will use the term average as a synonym for the mean and the term typical value to refer generically to measures of location.
</Callout>

Software backs the same convention. [Microsoft's documentation for the Excel AVERAGE function](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/average-function-047bac88-d466-426c-a32b-8f33eb960cf6) describes it as returning "the average (arithmetic mean)" of its arguments — not the median, not the mode.

The difference that remains is one of scope, not arithmetic. "Mean" names one specific calculation; "average" is the broader umbrella label that also covers the median and the mode. So a plain mean vs average comparison usually finds no daylight between the two numbers at all — but the label "average" can legally wear a median's or a weighted mean's clothes, which is why the mix-up matters a great deal the moment a headline reports an "average" that wasn't actually a mean. The cases where that happens are covered later in this guide.

Statistics keeps three average definitions because summarizing data always throws information away on purpose, and each of the three protects a different property of the original numbers.

## What Is a Sample Mean vs a Population Mean?

A **population mean (μ)** covers every member of a defined group — every invoice a company issued last year, every seed in one batch. A **sample mean (x̄)**, the figure behind most of what gets called mean statistics in practice, covers only a subset of that group, used to estimate μ when measuring the whole population isn't practical.

```
x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n  =  Σxᵢ / n      (sample mean)
μ  = (X₁ + X₂ + … + X_N) / N  =  ΣXᵢ / N      (population mean)
```

The arithmetic is identical either way — sum, divide by count, whether you call the count n or N — only the symbol and the claim behind it change. A sample mean is a **statistic**: recompute it from a different sample drawn from the same population and you will likely get a slightly different number. A population mean is a **parameter**: one fixed, usually unknown, true value. Draw two different 5-invoice samples from the same 10,000-invoice population and you can easily get two different x̄ values — say $412 from one sample and $429 from the other — and both are legitimate estimates of the single, fixed μ that neither sample can see directly. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, 1.3.5.2 — Confidence Limits for the Mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm) builds directly on this distinction: it shows how to attach a confidence interval to x̄ precisely because a sample mean is only an estimate, not the population mean itself.

Almost all the data anyone works with day to day is a sample, which is why x̄ shows up far more often in practice than μ — and why careful writing reports x̄ as an *estimate* of μ, never as if the two were guaranteed to match. The <Link to={routes.article('standard-deviation-symbol')}>standard deviation symbol guide</Link> covers the parallel σ-versus-s notation used for spread.

## What Is a Grand Average?

A **grand average** (or grand mean) is the average of several *group* averages rather than of the raw individual values — for example, averaging three factories' output figures into one company-wide number. It matters mainly for one reason: you can only combine group averages directly when every group is the same size. The moment group sizes differ, a plain average of averages quietly over- or under-credits whichever group happens to be a different size than the rest. <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-mean')}>How to find the mean</Link> works through exactly why, and by how much, using a delivery-route example you can check by hand.

## When Does "Average" Stop Meaning the Mean?

Four situations regularly produce an "average" that is not the plain arithmetic mean. [Manikandan's overview of measures of central tendency](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3127352/) lists the weighted, geometric, and harmonic means right alongside the arithmetic one for exactly this reason — each is a genuine type of mean, just not the default one — and each produces a plausible-looking wrong answer below if you just sum and divide anyway.

**A skewed dataset.** A courier logs nine delivery times, in minutes, for one afternoon route: 18, 22, 19, 25, 21, 20, 23, 19, and 145 — the last one stuck behind a road closure. The mean is (18+22+19+25+21+20+23+19+145) / 9 = 312 / 9 ≈ 34.7 minutes, but the median (the 5th of the nine sorted values) is 21 minutes. Drop the outlier and the other eight deliveries average 20.9 minutes — close to the median. The single stuck delivery drags the mean up by two-thirds; it barely nudges the median, which only cares about rank. That gap is a measure of **skewness**, which the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, 1.3.5.11 — Measures of Skewness and Kurtosis](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35b.htm) defines as "a measure of symmetry, or more precisely, the lack of symmetry." It's a diagnostic in its own right: per the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, 1.3.5.1 — Measures of Location](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm), "if the right tail is heavier than the left tail, the mean will be greater than the median" — exactly the signature behind "average income" headlines that outstrip what a typical household earns. <Link to={routes.article('median-vs-average')}>Median vs average</Link> goes deeper on when to make the switch.

**Values that carry different weight.** A weighted average multiplies each value xᵢ by a weight wᵢ before combining them:

```
x̄_w = Σ(wᵢ · xᵢ) / Σwᵢ
```

A woodworker sells hand-turned bowls at $40 (30 units) and $85 (10 units) in one month. The simple average price tag is (40 + 85) / 2 = $62.50, but plugging the unit counts in as weights gives the average price actually paid across every bowl sold: (40×30 + 85×10) / (30 + 10) = 2,050 / 40 = $51.25. Whenever quantities differ, "average" silently means "weighted mean," and a plain mean gives the wrong number. <Link to={routes.article('weighted-average')}>Weighted average</Link> covers the full formula and worked GPA and portfolio examples.

**A rate measured over equal counts, not equal time.** A backend service runs 600 requests at 100 requests/second, then another 600 requests at 300 requests/second. The naive average of the two rates is (100 + 300) / 2 = 200 req/s — but Phase 1 actually took 6 seconds and Phase 2 took 2 seconds, so the real throughput over the full 1,200 requests in 8 seconds is 150 req/s. That matches the harmonic mean, 2 / (1/100 + 1/300) = 150 req/s, not the naive arithmetic mean. The same trap catches average speed over equal distances: drive a route at 40 km/h, then 60 km/h, and your average speed for the whole trip is not 50 km/h.

**A sequence that compounds.** A small fund posts three consecutive annual returns: −10%, +20%, +15%. The naive average, (−10 + 20 + 15) / 3 ≈ 8.33% per year, implies $10,000 grows to roughly $10,000 × 1.0833³ ≈ $12,714. Walking the actual balance through year by year instead gives $10,000 × 0.90 = $9,000, then × 1.20 = $10,800, then × 1.15 = $12,420 — noticeably less. The number that reconstructs the real ending balance is the geometric mean of the three yearly multipliers, (0.90 × 1.20 × 1.15)^(1/3) ≈ 1.0749, or about 7.5% per year; multiplied out three times, 1.0749³ ≈ 1.242, matching $12,420 exactly.

## How Can You Tell Which Average a Source Actually Used?

A published "average" rarely comes labeled with the calculation behind it. Run through a few checks before treating one as the arithmetic mean.

- **Look for the word itself.** "Mean" and "average" usually signal the arithmetic calculation; "median," "typical," or "midpoint" signal something else. A report that says "median household income" is being deliberate, not sloppy.
- **Check whether the topic is known to skew.** Income, home prices, wait times, and salaries are almost always right-skewed in the real world. An unlabeled "average" attached to one of these topics is worth treating with suspicion.
- **Look at how the units are counted.** If an "average" combines groups of very different sizes — average price across product lines with wildly different sales volumes — it is very likely a weighted average, not a simple mean of group-level numbers.
- **Check whether it's a rate or a growth figure.** "Average speed," "average return," and "average growth rate" are candidates for the harmonic or geometric mean rather than the arithmetic one.
- **When the source genuinely doesn't say**, ask for the median alongside the mean, or the sample size behind any weighted figure. A single unlabeled "average" isn't enough information to know whether you're looking at a typical case or a number distorted by a handful of extreme values.

## A Worked Example: One Shop, Three Averages

Mara runs PrintKind, a one-person Etsy shop that 3D-prints custom keychains. On Monday she shipped 11 orders; the number of keychains in each order was:

```
3, 4, 3, 5, 3, 12, 4, 3, 5, 4, 3
```

Order six was a wholesale request from a wedding planner buying party favors; the rest were ordinary retail orders.

1. **Compute the mean.** Add all eleven order sizes and divide by 11.

```
Sum = 3+4+3+5+3+12+4+3+5+4+3 = 49
Mean = 49 / 11 ≈ 4.45 keychains per order
```

2. **Compute the median.** Sort the same eleven values: 3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 12. With 11 values, the middle position is (11 + 1) / 2 = 6th, which lands on **4**.

3. **Compute the mode.** Count how often each size shows up: 3 appears five times, 4 appears three times, 5 appears twice, and 12 appears once. The mode is **3**.

4. **Decide, per number.** To buy filament for next Monday, Mara needs the mean, because filament use scales with the *total* keychain count (4.45 × 11 ≈ 49, the real sum). To write "our typical order is about X keychains" on her FAQ page, the median (4) is the more honest number, since it isn't dragged upward by the one wedding order. To decide which packaging size to stock most of, the mode (3) is the actual answer — the single most common order she fills.

Drop the wedding order and the ten remaining orders are 3, 4, 3, 5, 3, 4, 3, 5, 4, 3. The mean becomes (49 − 12) / 10 = 3.7; the median becomes the average of the 5th and 6th sorted values, (3 + 4) / 2 = 3.5; the mode stays exactly 3, unmoved, because removing the single "12" changed nothing about which value repeats most. One outlier shifted the mean by 0.75 — a 17% swing — nudged the median by only 0.5, and left the mode untouched. That ordering, from most sensitive to least, holds for outliers in general.

## What Mistakes Do People Make With the Word "Average"?

These are mix-ups about what kind of claim "average" is making, before any arithmetic even happens.

- **Assuming "average" always means "mean."** It's the safest guess, but not a certainty — a report on "average income" is frequently a median, chosen precisely because income is skewed.
- **Averaging percentages or rates without weighting.** If 80% of 200 customers in one region are satisfied and 40% of 20 customers in another are, the combined rate is not (80 + 40) / 2 = 60%. It's (0.80×200 + 0.40×20) / 220 ≈ 76.4% — the larger group should dominate, and a naive average of the two percentages ignores that entirely.
- **Comparing two groups' averages when the counts behind them differ wildly.** A clinic that treats 5 patients and reports an average recovery time of 6 days is not directly comparable to one that treats 500 patients and reports 9 days — the first average can swing on a single unusual case; the second is far more stable.
- **Forgetting the mode exists for categorical data.** If you survey 200 people about their favorite movie genre, you cannot compute a mean genre — there's no numeric scale to sum. Per a [peer-reviewed overview of the median and mode](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3157145/), the mode is "the only measure of central tendency that can be used for data measured in a nominal scale," which is exactly this case.
- **Reading "on average" as a promise for every individual case.** "Patients recover, on average, in 12 days" describes the center of a distribution, not a floor or a ceiling.
- **Assuming two datasets with the same average look alike.** Ten patients recovering in exactly 12 days each, and ten recovering anywhere from 2 to 30 days, can share the identical mean of 12. The average tells you the center; the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, 1.3.5.6 — Measures of Scale](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda356.htm) covers the separate question of how far values scatter around that center, and the <Link to={routes.article('standard-deviation')}>standard deviation</Link> guide works through that formula with a worked example.

## Statohub's Take on What "Average" Means

Statohub's position is that "average" is a useful word precisely because it's vague, and a dangerous one for the same reason. It's fine as a conversational placeholder; it's not precise enough to report without naming the calculation behind it. The single habit that prevents most of the mistakes on this page is cheap: whenever you write or read the word "average," ask which of the three — or five, once weighting, rates, and compounding enter the picture — actually produced the number.
