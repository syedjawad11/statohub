The correlation definition at the center of statistics is simple: correlation is a numerical measure of how strongly two quantitative variables move together, and in which direction. If you only need the correlation def in one line, that's it — everything else on this page is detail. The standard tool for measuring it is Pearson's r, a number between −1 and +1 that captures both the strength and the direction of a linear relationship. Grasping the correlation meaning behind that number — rather than just its sign — is what lets you read a scatterplot, a correlation table, or a research paper's "r = 0.42" without misreading it.

## What Does the Correlation Definition Cover?

Correlation describes association, not causation: two variables are correlated when knowing the value of one tells you something about the likely value of the other. The most widely used measure, Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient (r), reduces that association to a single number bounded between −1 and +1.

A scatterplot is the fastest way to confirm what a correlation number is telling you. The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's Scatter Plot entry](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/scatterp.htm) describes the scatterplot as "the most important and most heavily used graphical tool for uncovering the existence and nature of relationships between variables" — exactly because r alone can hide a relationship's true shape. Software built around correlation treats it the same way: [SciPy's `pearsonr` function](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.pearsonr.html) returns both the r statistic and a p-value testing whether that r is distinguishable from zero, and [Minitab's correlation documentation](https://support.minitab.com/en-us/minitab/help-and-how-to/statistics/basic-statistics/how-to/correlation/methods-and-formulas/methods-and-formulas/) lays out the same Pearson formula alongside a confidence interval for the population correlation. For the full derivation and a hand-worked calculation, see the <Link to={routes.article('correlation-coefficient')}>correlation coefficient guide</Link>; this page stays with the concept.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
Treat the correlation coefficient as a summary, not a verdict. Two datasets can share the exact same r and look completely different on a scatterplot — always plot before you report.
</Callout>

## What Is Positive Correlation?

Positive correlation means both variables move in the same direction: as one increases, the other tends to increase as well, and r sits between 0 and +1. A perfect positive correlation (r = +1) means every point falls exactly on an upward-sloping line — something real data essentially never produces, because measurement error and other variables always add some scatter.

Common positive correlation examples:

- **Study hours and exam score.** More preparation time tends to accompany a higher score (worked below).
- **Temperature and ice-cream sales.** Warmer days tend to bring more purchases; neither variable causes the other directly, but both move with the season.
- **Height and weight.** Taller people tend to weigh more, even though individual variation is large.
- **Advertising spend and revenue.** Higher marketing budgets often accompany higher sales, especially early in a product's life, before the relationship tends to flatten out.

## What Is Negative Correlation?

Negative correlation means the variables move in opposite directions: as one increases, the other tends to decrease, and r sits between −1 and 0. The scatterplot slopes downward from left to right.

Common negative correlation examples:

- **Exercise frequency and resting heart rate.** More regular exercise tends to go with a lower resting heart rate, reflecting better cardiovascular conditioning.
- **Vehicle age and resale value.** Older cars generally sell for less, as wear and newer model-year features pull the price down.
- **Class absences and exam score.** Students who miss more sessions tend to score lower.
- **Altitude and the boiling point of water.** Water boils at a lower temperature at higher altitude, because atmospheric pressure drops as altitude increases.

An r of −0.85 is exactly as strong a relationship as an r of +0.85 — only the direction differs. Neither sign makes a correlation more or less "real"; a researcher deciding which variable to plot on which axis has no effect on the strength of the relationship, only on how it looks visually.

## What Does Zero Correlation Mean?

A correlation near 0 means Pearson's r finds no straight-line pattern between the two variables — not that the variables have nothing to do with each other. Two variables can follow a strong curved relationship (a U-shape, for instance) and still return r ≈ 0, because the formula only measures linear association. That's the single biggest trap in interpreting correlation: a low r rules out a straight-line relationship, but it never rules out a relationship.

<Callout variant="quote" source="NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods" sourceHref="https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/scatterp.htm">
Scatter plots reveal association, which is step one toward cause-and-effect. There is no statistical procedure, the scatter plot included, that proves cause-and-effect.
</Callout>

Because r misses non-linear structure, pair any zero or near-zero correlation with a scatterplot before concluding the variables are unrelated. If a relationship clearly exists and you want to know whether it is causal, see <Link to={routes.article('correlation-vs-causation')}>correlation vs causation</Link> for how researchers tell the two apart.

## How Do You Find the Mean of Several Correlation Coefficients?

When you have Pearson correlations from several studies or subgroups, you cannot average the raw r values — Pearson's r is not measured on a linear scale, especially near ±1, so a straight arithmetic mean produces a biased, usually too-small, mean correlation. The fix is to move each r onto a scale where ordinary averaging is valid: Fisher's z-transformation maps the bounded [−1, +1] range onto an unbounded one, so you average in z-space and convert back. [R's `cor.test` documentation](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/cor.test.html) confirms this is the standard transform for Pearson correlations, noting that "if there are at least 4 complete pairs of observation, an asymptotic confidence interval is given based on Fisher's Z transform."

**Fisher's z-transformation:**

```
z' = 0.5 × ln[(1 + r) / (1 − r)]
```

**Worked example.** Two subgroups report r₁ = 0.50 and r₂ = 0.80.

```
z'1 = 0.5 × ln[(1 + 0.50) / (1 − 0.50)] = 0.5 × ln(3.00)  = 0.5 × 1.0986 = 0.5493
z'2 = 0.5 × ln[(1 + 0.80) / (1 − 0.80)] = 0.5 × ln(9.00)  = 0.5 × 2.1972 = 1.0986

z'_mean = (0.5493 + 1.0986) / 2 = 0.8240

r_mean = tanh(0.8240) ≈ 0.6772
```

The mean correlation is **r ≈ 0.68**. Averaging the raw values directly gives (0.50 + 0.80) / 2 = 0.65 — noticeably lower, because the r scale compresses as it approaches ±1. When sample sizes differ, weight each z' by (nᵢ − 3) before averaging so larger samples carry proportionally more influence:

```
z'_weighted = Σ[(nᵢ − 3) × z'ᵢ] / Σ(nᵢ − 3)
```

## What Other Correlation Coefficients Exist Beyond Pearson's r?

Pearson's r assumes both variables are continuous and the relationship is linear. When that assumption does not hold, three alternatives cover most other cases:

- **Spearman's rank correlation (rₛ)** ranks both variables first, then applies the Pearson formula to those ranks, which makes it robust to outliers and appropriate for relationships that are not strictly linear. [SciPy's `spearmanr` function](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.spearmanr.html) describes it as "a nonparametric measure of the monotonicity of the relationship between two datasets."
- **Kendall's tau (τ)**, implemented in [SciPy's `kendalltau` function](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.kendalltau.html), compares every possible pair of observations and measures the proportion that are concordant (both variables agree in ranking) minus the proportion that are discordant.
- **Point-biserial correlation** measures the relationship between one continuous variable and one binary (0/1) variable; mathematically, it is the Pearson formula applied directly to that pair.

All three stay on the same −1 to +1 scale as Pearson's r, so the sign-and-magnitude reading above still applies. The choice comes down to data type and whether a strictly linear relationship is reasonable to assume; see <Link to={routes.article('pearson-vs-spearman')}>Pearson vs Spearman</Link> for a full side-by-side comparison.

## How Do You Read a Correlation Table for a Positive Correlation?

A correlation table (or correlation matrix) lists pairwise Pearson r values for three or more variables at once; each cell shows the correlation between the row variable and the column variable. Software makes these routinely — [pandas' `DataFrame.corr` method](https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/reference/api/pandas.DataFrame.corr.html) returns the full matrix for every numeric column pair with a single call — so knowing how to read one matters as much as knowing how to compute one.

The main diagonal is always 1.00 (every variable correlates perfectly with itself), and the matrix is symmetric: the X–Y cell matches the Y–X cell. **Which table shows a positive correlation?** The one whose off-diagonal cells are clearly positive — here, X and Y (0.85) and Y and Z (0.30) both qualify, while X and Z (−0.12) is a weak negative pairing. As a rule of thumb, treat off-diagonal values of roughly +0.3 or higher as a meaningful positive correlation, values near −0.3 or lower as meaningful negative, and anything hovering near 0 as no linear relationship. Larger matrices (five or more variables) are read the same way — scan the off-diagonal half, note the sign, then the magnitude.

## A Worked Example: Study Hours and Exam Scores

A teacher records weekly study hours (x) and end-of-term exam scores (y) for five students:

| Student | Study hours (x) | Exam score (y) |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|
| A | 2 | 60 |
| B | 3 | 65 |
| C | 4 | 70 |
| D | 5 | 80 |
| E | 6 | 85 |

1. **Find each mean.** Mean study hours = (2+3+4+5+6)/5 = 4. Mean score = (60+65+70+80+85)/5 = 72.
2. **Multiply each pair's deviations and sum them.** Σ(x − 4)(y − 72) = (−2)(−12) + (−1)(−7) + (0)(−2) + (1)(8) + (2)(13) = 24 + 7 + 0 + 8 + 26 = 65.
3. **Find each variable's spread.** √Σ(x − 4)² = √10 ≈ 3.1623; √Σ(y − 72)² = √430 ≈ 20.7364.
4. **Divide.** r = 65 / (3.1623 × 20.7364) ≈ 65 / 65.5744 ≈ **0.9912**.

Every student who studied more scored higher, and the increases are close to proportional — r ≈ 0.99 is nearly a perfect positive correlation. Squaring r gives r² ≈ 0.983, meaning about 98% of the variance in exam score lines up with the linear trend in study hours; the <Link to={routes.article('r-squared-adjusted-r-squared')}>r-squared guide</Link> covers how to read that percentage in a larger dataset with more than one predictor.

5. **Check whether r is reliable at this sample size.** With only 5 students, a strong-looking r can still be a fluke, so test it: [R's `cor.test` documentation](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/cor.test.html) tests whether a sample correlation is distinguishable from zero using a t-statistic with n − 2 degrees of freedom, t = r × √(n − 2) / √(1 − r²).

```
df = n − 2 = 3
t = 0.9912 × √3 / √(1 − 0.9912²)
  = 0.9912 × 1.7321 / √(1 − 0.9826)
  = 1.7169 / √0.0174
  = 1.7169 / 0.1321
  ≈ 13.00
```

6. **Compare t to the critical value and decide.** With 3 degrees of freedom, the two-tailed critical value at α = 0.05 is 3.182. The computed t (13.00) is far larger than that threshold, so this correlation is statistically distinguishable from zero even in this small class.

**Decision:** r ≈ 0.99, r² ≈ 0.98, and a t of 13.00 against a critical value of 3.182 together support treating study hours as a strong linear predictor of this exam score — enough to recommend more structured study time to this class. The sample is still only 5 students, though, so before applying the same conclusion to a full cohort, this result is worth replicating with a larger group; an r of 0.80 from 8 observations and an r of 0.80 from 200 observations are not equally trustworthy, even when the number itself is identical. ## Common Mistakes When Interpreting Correlation

- **Treating zero correlation as "no relationship."** Pearson's r only catches linear patterns; a strong curve can produce r ≈ 0. Plot the data before ruling anything out, and if the shape looks curved, consider a non-linear model instead of concluding the variables are unconnected.
- **Averaging raw r values across studies.** Convert each r to Fisher's z, average the z values, then convert back — never average r directly, since the raw scale compresses as it nears ±1 and biases the result downward.
- **Reading correlation as causation.** A high r never proves one variable causes the other; both may be driven by a confounder, or the link may be coincidental. See <Link to={routes.article('correlation-vs-causation')}>correlation vs causation</Link> for how to tell them apart.
- **Ignoring sample size.** An r of 0.80 from eight observations carries far less weight than the same r from 200. [R's `cor.test`](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/cor.test.html) tests r against zero using n − 2 degrees of freedom precisely because small samples make any r less trustworthy — report n every time you report r.
- **Assuming one correlation fits every subgroup.** The [NIST/SEMATECH Linear Correlation Plot](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/linecorr.htm) exists specifically to check whether a single correlation is consistent across groups, or whether each group needs its own; pooling groups with genuinely different relationships can hide the real pattern in each one.
- **Letting one outlier set the story.** A single extreme point can pull r toward ±1 or toward 0; check for outliers before reporting a correlation as final, and note in your write-up whether the result changes with that point removed.
- **Ignoring range restriction.** If your sample only covers part of x's natural spread — say, only applicants who already scored above a cutoff — r will look weaker than it would across the full range, because restricting the range of one variable mechanically shrinks the correlation you can observe.

## Statohub's Take on the Correlation Definition

Statohub's position is that correlation is the most under-read number in applied statistics — not because the correlation definition is hard, but because the sign and the scatterplot get skipped in favor of the single number. Report r alongside its sign, its sample size, and a plot of the data, and most of the common misreadings above disappear on their own. The correlation meaning behind a given r changes with field and sample size, so treat any fixed threshold for "strong" or "weak" as a starting point for discussion, not a verdict to cite without context.
