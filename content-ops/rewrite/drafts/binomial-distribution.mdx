The **binomial distribution** gives the exact probability of counting a certain number of successes across a fixed number of independent trials, when every trial carries the same success probability. You meet it whenever you flip a coin ten times and count the heads, test thirty patients who each either respond to a drug or do not, or inspect a batch of parts that each either pass or fail. Unlike an average, the binomial distribution hands you a full probability for every possible count — 0 successes, 1 success, 2 successes, all the way to n. This guide works through the conditions that make a variable binomial, the binomial distribution formula and the related binomial distribution formulas for cumulative probability, how Bernoulli's distribution anchors the whole model, where the geometric distribution fits alongside it, and two fully worked numeric examples.

Before you reach for the binomial distribution formula, check that your problem really satisfies all four conditions. Skipping this step is the single most common reason a binomial calculation turns out wrong.

If any condition fails, a different model usually fits better — sampling without replacement from a finite pool calls for the <Link to={routes.article('hypergeometric-distribution')}>hypergeometric distribution</Link>, and counting rare events over a continuous span of time or space calls for the <Link to={routes.article('poisson-distribution')}>Poisson distribution</Link> instead.

## What Is the Binomial Distribution?

A random variable X follows a binomial distribution when four conditions hold at once: a fixed number of trials n, exactly two mutually exclusive outcomes per trial ("success" and "failure"), a constant success probability p on every trial, and independence between trials. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/4-3-binomial-distribution) lists these same four requirements before introducing the notation `X ~ B(n, p)`, read "X follows a binomial distribution with parameters n and p," where X can take any integer value from 0 to n.

The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's binomial distribution entry](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm) frames it the same way: the binomial model gives the probability of observing x successes in N trials, with p fixed across every trial. It calls the binomial "probably the most commonly used discrete distribution" — a fair label, given how often real decisions reduce to counting yes/no outcomes across a known number of attempts.

<Callout variant="quote" source="NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods" sourceHref="https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm">
The binomial distribution is probably the most commonly used discrete distribution.
</Callout>

The name itself is a hint at the mechanics: "bi-" for the two possible outcomes per trial, and "-nomial" for the terms being summed across trials. That makes the binomial distribution inherently **discrete** — X can only take whole-number values from 0 to n, never a fraction of a success — which is a different kind of object from a continuous model like the <Link to={routes.article('normal-distribution')}>normal distribution</Link>, where any real number in a range is possible.

## What Is a Bernoulli Trial, and How Does Bernoulli's Distribution Fit In?

A single trial with only two possible outcomes — success with probability p, failure with probability `1 − p` — is called a **Bernoulli trial**, after the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli, whose posthumously published 1713 work *Ars Conjectandi* laid foundational groundwork for probability theory, according to [the MacTutor History of Mathematics archive at the University of St Andrews](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Bernoulli_Jacob/).

**Bernoulli's distribution** is simply the special case of the binomial where n = 1:

```
P(X = 1) = p          (success)
P(X = 0) = 1 − p      (failure)
```

Its mean is p and its variance is `p(1 − p)`. The link to the full binomial model is direct: a binomial variable with parameters n and p is nothing more than the sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) trials. Flip a coin ten times, and each flip is its own Bernoulli(0.5) trial; the running total of heads is `B(10, 0.5)`. Some courses present Bernoulli's distribution and the binomial distribution as two separate topics, but once you see the sum relationship, the two sets of formulas become the same formula at n = 1 versus general n.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
If a problem description says "a single trial" or "one attempt," you're looking at Bernoulli's distribution, not the general binomial — check whether n actually equals 1 before reaching for the full formula.
</Callout>

## What Is the Binomial Distribution Formula?

The **binomial distribution formula** gives the probability of exactly k successes across n trials, each with success probability p:

```
P(X = k) = C(n, k) × p^k × (1 − p)^(n − k)
```

Here `n` is the total number of trials, `k` is the specific count of successes you're asking about (an integer from 0 to n), `p` is the probability of success on one trial, and `C(n, k)` is the **binomial coefficient** — "n choose k" — which counts how many distinct ways k successes can be arranged among n trials:

```
C(n, k) = n! / (k! × (n − k)!)
```

The formula multiplies three separate pieces of information. `C(n, k)` counts every valid arrangement of which trials succeed. `p^k` is the probability that each of those k chosen trials actually succeeds. `(1 − p)^(n − k)` is the probability that every remaining trial fails. Because the trials are independent, individual probabilities multiply; the binomial coefficient out front makes sure every arrangement gets counted exactly once rather than being double-counted or missed.

As a quick illustration: for n = 6 trials with p = 0.4, the probability of exactly k = 2 successes is `C(6, 2) × 0.4² × 0.6⁴ = 15 × 0.16 × 0.1296 ≈ 0.3110`, or about 31.1%. Working through C(n, k) by hand gets tedious once n grows past the teens; the <Link to={routes.calculator('combination')}>combination calculator</Link> computes it directly if you only need that one piece.

The best-known instance of this formula is probably the fair coin: for n = 10 flips with p = 0.5, `P(X = 5) = C(10, 5) × 0.5^10 = 252 / 1024 ≈ 0.2461`, so even the single most likely outcome — 5 heads out of 10 — still comes up less than 1 time in 4.

Two edge cases are worth checking by hand once, because they show the formula degenerating sensibly rather than breaking. At k = 0, `C(n, 0) = 1` and the formula collapses to `P(X = 0) = (1 − p)^n` — every single trial has to fail. At k = n, `C(n, n) = 1` as well, and `P(X = n) = p^n` — every trial has to succeed. Both match intuition directly: with n = 6 and p = 0.4, `P(X = 0) = 0.6^6 ≈ 0.0467` and `P(X = 6) = 0.4^6 ≈ 0.0041`, so getting all failures is roughly 11 times more likely than getting all successes when the success probability sits below 0.5.

## What Are the Mean, Variance, and Shape of a Binomial Distribution?

Three numbers — mean, variance, and standard deviation — summarize where a binomial distribution centers and how far it spreads, and two more describe its shape.

```
Mean:                μ = n × p
Variance:            σ² = n × p × (1 − p)
Standard deviation:  σ = √(n × p × (1 − p))
```

These fall out of the Bernoulli building blocks directly: each Bernoulli(p) trial contributes mean p and variance `p(1 − p)`, and because the n trials are independent, you sum both the means and the variances across all of them. The [NIST/SEMATECH handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm) also documents the distribution's skewness, `(1 − 2p) / √(np(1 − p))`, and kurtosis, `3 − 6/n + 1/(np(1 − p))` — useful for judging how lopsided a given binomial distribution looks before you lean on a symmetric approximation.

Kurtosis close to 3 means a binomial distribution's peak and tails behave roughly like a normal curve's. Both correction terms in the formula shrink as n grows, so kurtosis tends toward 3 for any fixed p; which side it approaches from depends on p itself — for moderate p (roughly 0.21 < p < 0.79, where `p(1 − p) > 1/6`) it sits just below 3, and for p near 0 or 1 it sits above 3. For n = 20 and p = 0.3, kurtosis comes out to roughly 2.94 — just below 3, consistent with that rule, and close enough that the shape is already fairly bell-like despite being a discrete, bounded distribution.

That positive skewness number is worth pausing on: whenever p is below 0.5, the binomial distribution leans slightly right, with a longer tail of higher-than-average counts; when p is above 0.5, it leans left instead. Only at p = 0.5 is the distribution exactly symmetric. The <Link to={routes.article('skewed-distribution')}>skewed distribution guide</Link> covers how to read and measure that lean in general, beyond this one discrete case.

## How Do You Calculate Cumulative Binomial Probabilities?

Most real questions don't ask for one exact count — they ask "at most," "at least," or "between." The **binomial distribution formulas** for these cases all build on the same exact-probability term, just summed differently.

<DataTable
  caption="The three binomial distribution formulas"
  headers={['Question', 'Formula', 'Reads as']}
  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[
    ['Exactly k', 'P(X = k) = C(n, k) × pᵏ × (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ', 'Probability mass function'],
    ['At most k', 'P(X ≤ k) = Σ P(X = i) for i = 0 … k', 'Cumulative distribution function'],
    ['At least k', 'P(X ≥ k) = 1 − P(X ≤ k − 1)', 'Survival function'],
  ]}
/>

"Exactly 5" and "at most 5" are not the same question and do not share an answer — confusing them is the most common error in a binomial calculation. "At most k" sums every term from 0 through k; "at least k" is easiest found by subtracting "at most k − 1" from 1, rather than summing a long upper tail directly.

**Example.** For 10 fair coin flips (n = 10, p = 0.5), what's the probability of 3 or fewer heads? Sum P(X = 0) through P(X = 3), each sharing the common factor `(0.5)^10 = 1/1024`:

```
P(X = 0) = C(10, 0) × (0.5)^10 =   1 / 1024
P(X = 1) = C(10, 1) × (0.5)^10 =  10 / 1024
P(X = 2) = C(10, 2) × (0.5)^10 =  45 / 1024
P(X = 3) = C(10, 3) × (0.5)^10 = 120 / 1024

P(X ≤ 3) = (1 + 10 + 45 + 120) / 1024 = 176 / 1024 ≈ 0.1719
```

There's roughly a **17.2% probability** of 3 or fewer heads in 10 flips. The complementary "at least" question — the probability of 4 or more heads — is simply `1 − 0.1719 = 0.8281`, found without summing seven more terms directly.

For large n, summing by hand gets impractical fast. A useful shortcut: when both `n × p ≥ 5` and `n × (1 − p) ≥ 5`, the binomial distribution is well approximated by a <Link to={routes.article('normal-distribution')}>normal distribution</Link> with the same mean and variance, with a ±0.5 continuity correction applied to each integer boundary. For small n or p near 0 or 1, that approximation breaks down and the exact binomial formula — or a <Link to={routes.article('z-table')}>z-table</Link> lookup after the normal approximation is actually valid — is the safer route.

## A Worked Example: Inspecting a Batch of Light Bulbs

**Setup.** A factory's defect rate is 10% per bulb (p = 0.10), independent from bulb to bulb. An inspector pulls a sample of n = 20 bulbs. What is the probability that exactly 2 are defective?

    steps={[

      { label: 'Compute C(n, k)', detail: 'C(20, 2) = 20! / (2! × 18!) = 190.' },
      { label: 'Compute the probability factors', detail: '(0.10)² = 0.01; (0.90)¹⁸ ≈ 0.1501.' },
      { label: 'Multiply everything together', detail: 'P(X = 2) = 190 × 0.01 × 0.1501 ≈ 0.2852.' },
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

1. **Compute the binomial coefficient.**

```
C(20, 2) = 20! / (2! × 18!) = (20 × 19) / 2 = 190
```

2. **Compute the two probability factors.**

```
(0.10)² = 0.01
(0.90)^18 ≈ 0.1501
```

3. **Multiply all three pieces together.**

```
P(X = 2) = 190 × 0.01 × 0.1501 ≈ 0.2852
```

**Decision.** There is roughly a **28.5% probability** of finding exactly 2 defective bulbs in this sample — the single most likely outcome for this scenario, since `μ = 20 × 0.10 = 2` defectives on average, with `σ = √(20 × 0.10 × 0.90) ≈ 1.34`. A sample that turned up, say, 7 or 8 defectives would sit roughly 3.7 to 4.5 standard deviations above that average — reason enough to suspect the 10% defect-rate assumption itself, not just bad luck.

## How Does the Geometric Distribution Differ From the Binomial Distribution?

The **geometric distribution** is built from the same Bernoulli trials as the binomial, but it answers a different question entirely. Instead of fixing n and counting successes, the geometric distribution counts how many trials it takes to reach the **first** success, with no fixed stopping point in advance. [OpenStax's *Introductory Statistics 2e*](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/4-4-geometric-distribution) defines its probability mass function as:

```
P(X = k) = (1 − p)^(k − 1) × p     for k = 1, 2, 3, ...
```

where k is the trial number on which the first success lands. Its mean is `1/p` and its variance is `(1 − p)/p²` — both grow as p shrinks, which makes sense: a rarer success means a longer expected wait.

**Example.** A salesperson closes 20% of calls (p = 0.2). What's the probability the first sale happens on exactly the 5th call?

```
P(X = 5) = (0.8)^4 × 0.2 = 0.4096 × 0.2 ≈ 0.0819
```

About an **8.2% probability** the first sale lands precisely on call 5. The expected number of calls until that first sale is `1/0.2 = 5` calls, and the probability the first sale happens somewhere within the first 5 calls is `1 − (1 − p)^5 = 1 − 0.8^5 ≈ 0.6723`, or 67.2%.

Both distributions need independent Bernoulli trials with a constant p — the distinction is entirely in what gets counted. "Flip 10 times and count heads" is binomial; "flip until the first head and count the flips" is geometric. The geometric distribution is also the discrete sibling of the <Link to={routes.article('exponential-distribution')}>exponential distribution</Link>: both model a wait for a first event and both carry the memoryless property, where the probability of waiting further doesn't depend on how long you've already waited.

The geometric distribution shows up anywhere the question is "how long until the first X," rather than "how many Xs in a fixed window." A quality-control line asking how many units get inspected before the first defect turns up, a subscription business asking how many billing cycles pass before a customer's first cancellation, and a search algorithm's expected number of probes before the first match in a random list are all geometric setups rather than binomial ones — none of them fix n in advance.

<Callout variant="note" title="Watch the parameterization">
R's `dgeom()` and `pgeom()` count the number of *failures* before the first success (support starting at 0), not the trial number of the first success (support starting at 1) used above and in Python's SciPy. The two conventions differ by exactly 1 — mixing them up silently shifts every answer.
</Callout>

## What Are Real-World Applications of the Binomial Distribution?

The binomial model shows up anywhere trials are independent, outcomes are binary, and the success probability holds steady:

- **Clinical trials.** If a treatment has a 60% response rate and is given to 30 patients, the binomial distribution gives the probability that at least 20 respond, which feeds directly into study-power calculations.
- **Quality control.** Acceptance-sampling plans use cumulative binomial probabilities to decide whether a production lot passes or fails inspection based on a sample count of defects.
- **Genetics.** Under Mendelian inheritance, a cross between two heterozygous parents gives each offspring a fixed 3/4 probability of the dominant phenotype — independent trials with constant p, a textbook binomial setup.
- **Polling.** When a sample is small relative to the population, binary survey answers (agree/disagree) behave like independent Bernoulli trials, and the binomial model governs the resulting counts.
- **Finance.** Binomial-tree option-pricing models assume a stock price moves up with probability p or down with probability `1 − p` at each discrete step.
- **A/B testing.** Each website visitor either converts or doesn't on a fixed-size sample of traffic, so the binomial distribution underlies the significance tests used to compare conversion rates between two page variants.

Not every counting problem is binomial, though. Drawing cards from a deck without replacement, or inspecting every unit in a small finite lot, breaks the independence condition — that's <Link to={routes.article('hypergeometric-distribution')}>hypergeometric distribution</Link> territory instead. And counting rare events over a continuous stretch of time or space, like customer arrivals per hour, fits the <Link to={routes.article('poisson-distribution')}>Poisson distribution</Link> better, since there's no natural "n" to fix in advance.

## How Do You Calculate Binomial and Geometric Probabilities in Excel, Python, and R?

```
Excel:   =BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, FALSE)     exact P(X = k)
         =BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, TRUE)      cumulative P(X <= k)

Python:  from scipy.stats import binom, geom
         binom.pmf(k, n, p)               # exact P(X = k)
         binom.cdf(k, n, p)                # cumulative P(X <= k)
         geom.pmf(k, p)                    # geometric, trial-count form, k >= 1

R:       dbinom(k, size = n, prob = p)     # exact P(X = k)
         pbinom(k, size = n, prob = p)     # cumulative P(X <= k)
         dgeom(x, prob = p)                # geometric, failure-count form, x >= 0
```

[Microsoft's documentation for `BINOM.DIST`](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/binom-dist-function-c5ae37b6-f39c-4be2-94c2-509a1480770c) confirms the fourth argument switches between the exact probability mass function and the cumulative distribution function. [SciPy's `scipy.stats.binom` reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.binom.html) and [`scipy.stats.geom` reference](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.geom.html) document the `pmf`, `cdf`, and `sf` methods for each. [R's documentation for the binomial distribution functions](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/Binomial.html) and [the geometric distribution functions](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/Geometric.html) cover `dbinom`/`pbinom`/`qbinom` and `dgeom`/`pgeom`/`qgeom` respectively — and flag the failure-count parameterization called out in the callout above.

## Common Mistakes When Using the Binomial Distribution

- **Using the exact formula when a cumulative one is needed.** "Exactly 5" and "at most 5" are different quantities with different formulas; read the question's wording ("exactly," "at most," "fewer than," "at least") before picking one.
- **Assuming independence when sampling without replacement.** Drawing items from a finite pool without replacing them changes p after each draw. The <Link to={routes.article('hypergeometric-distribution')}>hypergeometric distribution</Link> is the correct model unless the sample is small relative to the population (a common rule of thumb: under 10%).
- **Treating Bernoulli's distribution as unrelated to the binomial.** It's the n = 1 case, full stop — the formulas are identical once you substitute n = 1.
- **Conflating the geometric and binomial distributions.** Fixed n and counting successes is binomial; no fixed n and counting trials to the first success is geometric. The formulas, means, and variances for the two are genuinely different.
- **Misapplying the normal approximation.** It needs both `n × p ≥ 5` and `n × (1 − p) ≥ 5` to be reliable; for small n or extreme p, use the exact binomial formula or the calculator above instead.
- **Forgetting that p must stay fixed across trials.** If a real-world success probability drifts — a machine wearing down over a production run, a sales rep improving with practice — the binomial model no longer applies cleanly, even though the trials still look independent and binary on the surface.
- **Mixing up the two geometric distribution parameterizations.** Python's SciPy counts the trial number of the first success starting at 1; R's base functions count the number of failures before it, starting at 0. Confirm which convention a tool uses before trusting its mean or variance output.

## Statohub's Take on the Binomial Distribution

Statohub's position is that the binomial distribution earns its reputation as the workhorse discrete model honestly — four conditions, one formula, and a huge share of real binary-outcome problems satisfy all four. The discipline worth building is checking those four conditions explicitly before calculating, and reading the question's wording carefully enough to know whether it's asking for an exact count or a cumulative one. Both habits prevent the two most common binomial errors long before any arithmetic happens.
