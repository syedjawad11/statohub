Median vs average is a comparison that trips people up because the two words don't actually describe the same kind of center. The average — almost always shorthand for the arithmetic mean — adds up every value in a data set and divides by how many values there are. The median instead sorts the data from smallest to largest and reports the value sitting in the exact middle, so it only cares about position, not size. On symmetric data the two numbers land close together; on skewed data or data with outliers, they can tell very different stories about what's "typical."

## Median vs Average: What's the Real Difference?

The average (mean) is the balance point of a data set; the median is its count midpoint. They measure "center" in genuinely different ways, which is why the same data set can hand you two different numbers depending on which one you report.

Think of each value as a weight sitting on a number line. The mean is the fulcrum that keeps the line level — because every weight contributes to the balance, one very heavy weight on either end shifts the fulcrum noticeably in that direction. The median, by contrast, only answers a counting question: what value splits the sorted list so that exactly half the observations fall on each side? A very large value can push the median one position over at most — it can never pull it past every value in the middle.

## What Does Median Mean in Math?

In math, the median is the value sitting at the exact midpoint of a sorted data set — half the values fall at or below it, half fall at or above it. That single property makes it a resistant measure of center: an extreme value at either end of the data cannot pull it far from where the bulk of the data sits. [OpenStax's Introductory Statistics](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/2-5-measures-of-the-center-of-the-data) puts it plainly: "the median is generally a better measure of the center when there are extreme values or outliers because it is not affected by the precise numerical values of the outliers." That resistance is also why a box plot anchors its center line on the median rather than the mean — the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook's box plot entry](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/boxplot.htm) describes plotting a symbol at the median before drawing the box out to the surrounding quartiles.

The word comes from the Latin *medianus*, meaning "middle." Finding it is mechanical once the data is sorted: pick the single middle value for an odd count, or average the two middle values for an even count. Sorting first is the step people skip — picking the middle entry of an unsorted list produces a meaningless number.

An even count is worth seeing worked through once, because the result can surprise people new to it. Take the data set 3, 7, 12, 20 (n = 4, already sorted). The two middle values are 7 and 12, so the median is (7 + 12) / 2 = 9.5 — a number that never actually appears in the data. The mean of the same four values is (3 + 7 + 12 + 20) / 4 = 42 / 4 = 10.5. Because this small data set has no real outlier, the median (9.5) and the mean (10.5) sit close together, which is itself a useful check: when the two measures agree this closely, neither one is being distorted by an extreme value. The full step-by-step procedure for both the odd and even cases, including grouped data, lives in <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-median')}>how to find the median</Link>; this page is about when the median is the number worth reporting in the first place.

## Is the Median an Average, or Just the Mean?

Technically and mathematically, yes — the median is one type of average. In formal statistics, "average" is any measure of central tendency: a single value meant to represent the typical value in a data set. The mean, median, and mode are all averages under that definition.

People search this question in several nearly identical forms — is median average, is median an average, is median the average, is the median the average — and the honest answer depends on which definition of "average" you mean. In everyday speech, "the average" almost always refers specifically to the arithmetic mean, the sum divided by the count. When a news report says "the average home price rose to $420,000," it means the mean, not the median. So:

- **In formal statistics:** the median is classified as a type of average, alongside the mean and mode. This usage is correct and standard in textbooks.
- **In everyday language:** "the average" usually means the arithmetic mean specifically. The median is a different number, calculated a different way, and it often reports a different value.

The confusion has real stakes. When a company reports "average employee pay," the choice between mean and median can produce very different figures, as the worked example later in this guide shows. Always check which measure a source actually used before comparing it to anything else. For the arithmetic mean's own definition, formula, and the handful of other things "average" can mean, see <Link to={routes.article('what-is-an-average')}>What Is an Average?</Link>; for computing it by hand, see <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-mean')}>how to find the mean</Link>.

## What Does the Gap Between Median and Average Reveal About Skew?

Comparing the mean and median on the same data set is a fast way to read the shape of a distribution, without needing to look at a histogram.

- **Mean ≈ median:** the distribution is roughly symmetric, with data spread evenly on both sides of the center.
- **Mean above median:** the distribution is right-skewed — a few unusually high values are pulling the mean upward. Income and house-price data almost always look this way.
- **Mean below median:** the distribution is left-skewed — a few unusually low values are dragging the mean down. Scores on an unusually easy exam can look this way.

<Callout variant="quote" source="OpenStax, Introductory Statistics, 2.6 Skewness and the Mean, Median, and Mode" sourceHref="https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/2-6-skewness-and-the-mean-median-and-mode">
Therefore, when the distribution of data is skewed to the left, the mean is often less than the median. When the distribution is skewed to the right, the mean is often greater than the median.
</Callout>

A quick check with real numbers: the data set 2, 5, 7, 9, 12 is already sorted and roughly symmetric. The median is the middle (3rd) value, 7. The mean is the sum (2 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 12 = 35) divided by 5 values, which is also 7. Mean and median matching exactly is a strong signal of symmetry; the further apart they land, the more skewed the data likely is. For a deeper look at identifying and handling skew specifically, see <Link to={routes.article('skewed-distribution')}>skewed distributions explained</Link>.

## When Should You Use the Median Instead of the Average?

Reach for the median as your primary measure of center whenever one of these applies:

- **The data is skewed.** Income, house prices, wealth, and hospital lengths of stay typically follow right-skewed distributions, where the median describes the typical case far better than the mean.
- **Outliers are present.** The [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook defines an outlier](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc16.htm) as an observation that lies an abnormal distance from the rest of the data — a single very expensive house in a neighborhood, or one patient with an unusually long recovery, fits that definition and can make the mean misleading. The median barely moves in response.
- **The scale is ordinal.** When data is ranked (satisfaction scores from 1 to 5, for example) rather than measured on a true numeric scale, the median category is meaningful, but arithmetic on ranks is questionable.
- **You want to describe a "typical" person or case.** Wherever most values cluster in a range but a few extremes exist, the median is the more honest summary to lead with.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Pro tip">
When you suspect a data set has outliers but you still need a mean for further calculations, report both numbers side by side. A mean and median that are far apart is itself useful information for the reader, not just a footnote.
</Callout>

## When Should You Use the Average Instead of the Median?

The arithmetic mean is the better choice whenever:

- **The data is roughly symmetric and outlier-free.** Here the mean and median are close, and the mean is usually preferred because it uses every data point.
- **You need to recover a total.** If you know the mean and the sample size, Total = mean × n. The median cannot reconstruct a total this way.
- **Further statistics require it.** Standard deviation, variance, z-scores, t-tests, and regression are all built from the mean, not the median. Once you compute the mean, you can build out the rest of the spread picture with <Link to={routes.article('standard-deviation')}>standard deviation</Link>, written with the <Link to={routes.article('standard-deviation-symbol')}>sigma notation</Link> covered elsewhere, and with <Link to={routes.article('variance')}>variance</Link>, which squares those same deviations.

## How Do You Calculate the Median in Python, R, or Excel?

Every major tool returns the same number the hand calculation does — sort the values, then take the middle one or average the two middle ones — but each exposes it through its own function.

```
Python:  from statistics import median
         median([3, 7, 12, 20])      # 9.5

R:       median(c(3, 7, 12, 20))      # 9.5

Excel:   =MEDIAN(A1:A4)               # 9.5
```

[Python's `statistics` module](https://docs.python.org/3/library/statistics.html) implements `median()` to average the two middle values automatically when the input has an even length, matching the by-hand result above. [R's `median()` function](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/median.html) behaves the same way and accepts an `na.rm` argument for stripping missing values before the calculation runs. [Microsoft's MEDIAN function documentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/median-function-d0916313-4753-414c-8537-ce85bdd967d2) describes the same spreadsheet formula, which ignores empty cells and text automatically. None of the three tools will warn you if you feed them an unsorted or mixed-type list, so it is still worth sanity-checking a surprising result by hand.

## A Worked Example: Median vs Average at a Nine-Person Startup

A nine-person startup wants to describe typical pay to a prospective hire. Annual salaries, in thousands of dollars, are:

42, 45, 47, 48, 50, 52, 55, 58, 210

The last figure is the founder's salary.

**Step 1 — Sort the values.** Already sorted: 42, 45, 47, 48, 50, 52, 55, 58, 210.

**Step 2 — Find the median.** There are n = 9 values (odd). Middle position = (9 + 1) / 2 = 5. The 5th value is 50 — that is, $50,000.

**Step 3 — Find the mean.**

```
Sum = 42 + 45 + 47 + 48 + 50 + 52 + 55 + 58 + 210 = 607
Mean = 607 / 9 ≈ 67.44
```

That's about $67,444.

**Step 4 — Interpret.** The median of $50,000 reflects the pay of the employee sitting exactly in the middle of the sorted list: four people earn less, four earn more. The mean of roughly $67,444 is higher than eight of the nine salaries — it only looks that large because the founder's $210,000 salary pulls it upward. A candidate told "the average salary here is about $67,000" would form a misleading expectation about typical pay at the company.

## Common Mistakes People Make With Median vs Average

- **Treating "average" and "median" as interchangeable.** For symmetric, outlier-free data they're close and either works; for skewed real-world data — most income, healthcare, and pricing data — they differ meaningfully, and picking the wrong one can mislead a reader badly.
- **Forgetting to sort before finding the median.** Picking the middle position of an unsorted list gives a meaningless value. Sort first, always.
- **Using the mean on ordinal data.** Averaging survey responses coded 1–5 produces a number like 3.4 that corresponds to no real category. The median category, or the mode, is more defensible.
- **Confusing a measure of center with a measure of spread.** The median and mean describe where the data sits; they say nothing about how spread out it is. That's a separate question answered by tools like <Link to={routes.article('how-to-find-the-range')}>the range</Link> or standard deviation.
- **Ignoring sample size.** For very small samples (n = 3 or 4), both the median and mean can shift substantially with a single new observation. Report the sample size alongside either number so a reader can judge how reliable it is.
- **Confusing the median with the mode.** The median is the middle value; the mode is the most frequently occurring one. They can coincide — in 1, 2, 3, 3, 5 the mode and the median are both 3 — or they can differ completely. See <Link to={routes.article('mean-median-mode-range')}>mean, median, mode, and range explained together</Link> for how all three relate.

## Statohub's Take on Median vs Average

Statohub's position is that "average" is too useful a word to retire, but too ambiguous to use without a second thought. When the data might be skewed or might contain outliers, the median deserves to be the headline number, with the mean reported alongside it rather than instead of it. Readers deserve to know which measure of center they're looking at, not just a single unlabeled number called "the average."
