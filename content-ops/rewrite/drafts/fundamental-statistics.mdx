**Fundamental statistics** is the branch of mathematics concerned with collecting, summarizing, and drawing conclusions from numerical data. In plain terms, it's the set of tools that turns a pile of raw numbers — test scores, patient outcomes, website visits — into a defensible answer to a real question. Every more advanced technique, from a t-test to a regression model, is built on the same handful of fundamentals: a clear definition of what you're measuring, the mean and spread of your data, and a way to say how confident you are in what you found.

## What Is Statistics? A Working Definition

**Statistics**, in everyday use, carries two related but distinct meanings, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion.

**Statistics as a field of study** (uncountable noun): the discipline of collecting and analyzing data. "I am studying statistics" names the academic subject.

**Statistics as data values** (countable noun): the specific numbers produced from a dataset. "The statistics show a 12% rise in enrollment" names computed figures.

Either way, the **statistics definition** rests on the same idea. [OpenStax's Introductory Statistics 2e](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/1-1-definitions-of-statistics-probability-and-key-terms) defines the field as the study of "the collection, analysis, interpretation, and presentation of data" — which is exactly why so many fields, from medicine to manufacturing, depend on the same underlying toolkit.

Every statistics problem also starts by naming what you want to learn about: a **population** (the entire group of interest) versus a **sample** (a subset drawn from it). Because populations are usually too large to measure in full, a sample's numbers — called statistics — are used to estimate the population's numbers, called parameters. That distinction, including the different symbols each side uses (μ versus x̄, σ versus s), is its own topic; the <Link to={routes.article('parameter-vs-statistic')}>parameter vs statistic guide</Link> covers it in depth, and the <Link to={routes.article('statistics-symbols-cheatsheet')}>statistics symbols cheat sheet</Link> lists every matching pair in one place.

## What Are the Two Branches of Statistics?

All of statistics math splits into two branches, and knowing which one a piece of analysis belongs to tells you immediately what it is — and is not — claiming.

**Descriptive statistics** summarize the data you already have, with no claim beyond that dataset: a class average, a frequency table, a standard deviation. **Inferential statistics** go further, using a sample to estimate or test something about a larger population — a confidence interval, a hypothesis test, a regression model. That move only works because a sample mean follows a predictable pattern across repeated samples — the sampling distribution described by [OpenStax's chapter on the Central Limit Theorem for sample means](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/7-1-the-central-limit-theorem-for-sample-means-averages) — which is also the machinery behind the confidence intervals [NIST/SEMATECH explains in its e-Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc14.htm).

**Which situation involves descriptive statistics?** Any time you summarize data you already collected, rather than generalizing beyond it. A teacher reporting a class's average test score, a sports analyst reporting a player's season points-per-game, and a store manager building an hourly customer-arrival table are all doing descriptive work — the numbers describe only the group actually measured. The moment that teacher's average gets used to claim something about all students everywhere, the work has crossed into inferential territory. For the full mechanics of that split — how to tell the two apart, when a sample is big enough to infer from, and worked examples in research and business — see the dedicated guide to <Link to={routes.article('descriptive-vs-inferential-statistics')}>descriptive vs inferential statistics</Link>.

## What Is Statistics Math? The Core Calculations You Need

The calculations behind both branches are more accessible than the terminology suggests. Three groups of numbers answer almost every question a beginner dataset can raise.

  caption="The core statistics math, at a glance"
  headers={['Measure', 'What it answers', 'Formula']}
  align={['left', 'left', 'left']}
  rows={[
    ['Mean (x̄)', 'Where is the center?', '(x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n'],
    ['Median', 'What is the middle value?', 'Middle of the sorted data'],
    ['Mode', 'What value repeats most?', 'Most frequent value'],
    ['Range', 'How wide is the spread?', 'Maximum − minimum'],
    ['Sample variance (s²)', 'How far, on average, do values stray from the mean?', 'Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)'],
    ['Sample standard deviation (s)', 'Spread, in the data\'s own units', '√s²'],
    ['Population variance (σ²)', 'Spread when you have every value, not a sample', 'Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N'],
    ['Population standard deviation (σ)', 'Population spread, in the data\'s own units', '√(Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N)'],
    ['Z-score', 'How unusual is one value?', '(x − x̄) / s'],
  ]}
/>

[NIST/SEMATECH's e-Handbook of Statistical Methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm) — the full [e-Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/index.htm) covers exploratory data analysis in far more depth than this guide needs — defines the mean as simply the sum of the data divided by the count, with the same arithmetic for either row of the table above; only the symbol and the denominator change (μ and N for a population, x̄ and n for a sample). Its [section on measures of scale](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda356.htm) defines the sample variance with n − 1 in the denominator rather than n. [OpenStax explains why](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/2-7-measures-of-the-spread-of-the-data): dividing by the smaller number, n − 1, gives a better estimate of the population variance than dividing by n would, based on the underlying theory of how a sample's own mean sits closer to its own data points than the true population mean does. The worked example below, like almost every real dataset you'll encounter, is a sample, so it uses the sample row; the full reasoning behind the n − 1 correction and the population/sample symbol conventions lives in the <Link to={routes.article('population-vs-sample')}>population vs sample guide</Link> and the <Link to={routes.article('parameter-vs-statistic')}>parameter vs statistic guide</Link>.

The median and mode need no formula beyond sorting and counting, but they matter for a different reason: a mean is pulled toward extreme values, while a median is not. The <Link to={routes.article('types-of-variables')}>types of variables guide</Link> explains which of these measures even applies to a given dataset — a mean only makes sense for numeric data, while a mode works for categories too.

Compute these six numbers by hand on a small dataset at least once. Software will do it faster, but knowing the arithmetic is the only way to catch a result that's obviously wrong — a negative standard deviation, for instance, which should never happen.
</Callout>

## How Do You Interpret a Z-Score?

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a single value sits from the mean, and its sign tells you which direction: positive means above the mean, negative means below. As a rough reading guide, a z-score near 0 is typical, a magnitude up to about 1 is still ordinary, and a magnitude above about 2 is usually flagged as unusually high or low — the exact cutoff depends on the field and the distribution, but ±2 is a common starting rule of thumb. A z-score of +2, for instance, reads as unusually high; a z-score of −1 reads as somewhat below average but still unremarkable. The worked example below applies this reading to a real value step by step, and the dedicated <Link to={routes.article('z-score')}>z-score guide</Link> covers z-score charts, the standard normal distribution, and more worked examples in full.

## Why Does Fundamental Statistics Matter in Everyday Life?

Fundamental statistics matters because it is the toolkit behind decisions that affect you directly — whether a drug works, whether a poll is reliable, whether a business forecast is worth trusting. Here is where each branch shows up in practice.

**Medicine and public health.** A drug trial that reports an average recovery time for a treatment group and a placebo group starts as descriptive statistics — those two averages describe only the people enrolled. Deciding whether the difference between them is real, rather than due to chance, is an inferential step, and the resulting survival rates and efficacy figures that get published are statistical estimates, not certainties.

**Business and economics.** Market research uses descriptive statistics to summarize survey results, while forecasting relies on inferential methods to project demand. Mistaking a correlation in that data for a cause can be a costly error — the <Link to={routes.article('correlation-vs-causation')}>correlation vs causation guide</Link> covers that distinction directly.

**Government and policy.** The [U.S. Census Bureau's decennial census](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial-census/about.html) counts the entire population once a decade, producing parameters rather than estimates — a rare case where the whole population actually gets measured. Between censuses, agencies rely on sampling and the same inferential machinery covered above.

**Measurement quality.** A statistic is only as good as the data behind it. If the instrument producing the data isn't consistent, no formula downstream can fix that; the <Link to={routes.article('reliability-cronbachs-alpha-cohens-kappa')}>reliability guide</Link> covers how to check whether a measurement is trustworthy before you start computing anything from it.

## A Worked Example: From Raw Data to a Z-Score

Suppose you record the daily high temperatures (°C) in a city for seven days:

```
18, 21, 19, 25, 22, 20, 23
```

**Step 1 — Mean.**

```
x̄ = (18 + 21 + 19 + 25 + 22 + 20 + 23) / 7 = 148 / 7 ≈ 21.14 °C
```

**Step 2 — Median.** Sorted: 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 25. With seven values, the median is the 4th: **21 °C**.

**Step 3 — Range.**

```
Range = 25 − 18 = 7 °C
```

**Step 4 — Sample variance.** First, the squared deviation of each value from the mean (x̄ ≈ 21.14):

<DataTable
  caption="Squared deviations from the mean, day by day"
  headers={['Day', 'xᵢ', 'xᵢ − x̄', '(xᵢ − x̄)²']}
  align={['left', 'right', 'right', 'right']}
  rows={[
    ['1', '18', '−3.14', '9.88'],
    ['2', '21', '−0.14', '0.02'],
    ['3', '19', '−2.14', '4.59'],
    ['4', '25', '3.86', '14.88'],
    ['5', '22', '0.86', '0.73'],
    ['6', '20', '−1.14', '1.31'],
    ['7', '23', '1.86', '3.45'],
  ]}
/>

```
Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ≈ 34.86
s² = 34.86 / (7 − 1) = 34.86 / 6 ≈ 5.81
```

**Step 5 — Standard deviation.**

```
s = √5.81 ≈ 2.41 °C
```

The typical daily high varies about 2.4 °C from the weekly mean of 21.1 °C — a fairly narrow spread, consistent with a stable weather week.

**Step 6 — Z-score for the hottest day.** The hottest day was 25 °C.

```
z = (25 − 21.14) / 2.41 = 3.86 / 2.41 ≈ 1.60
```

That reading sits 1.60 standard deviations above the mean — noticeably warm, but not extreme. Applying the ±2 rule of thumb from the z-score guide above, 1.60 doesn't cross that line. **Decision: 25 °C does not count as an unusual day this week**; it's the warmest of the seven, but still within the range normal weekly variation produces. This one example touches every core measure covered above: center (mean, median), spread (range, variance, standard deviation), and relative position (z-score). For a deeper treatment of any single measure — including outlier handling and more worked examples — see the dedicated guides to the <Link to={routes.article('standard-deviation')}>standard deviation</Link>, <Link to={routes.article('variance')}>variance</Link>, and <Link to={routes.article('z-score')}>z-score</Link>.

## Common Mistakes When Learning Fundamental Statistics

- **Confusing the mean with the median.** The mean is pulled toward outliers; the median is not. In a skewed dataset the two can differ substantially, and reporting only the mean can misrepresent a "typical" value.
- **Treating a sample statistic as a population parameter.** A sample mean (x̄) is an *estimate* of the true population mean (μ), not the population mean itself. Forgetting that carries unearned confidence into a conclusion.
- **Assuming correlation implies causation.** Two variables can move together for reasons that have nothing to do with one causing the other; a third, unmeasured variable is often the real driver.
- **Ignoring units on variance.** Variance is reported in squared units; always take the square root (standard deviation) before stating a spread figure that anyone needs to interpret in context.
- **Using a descriptive statement to answer an inferential question.** "The treatment group improved more on average" describes the sample. Deciding whether that improvement is large enough to be *statistically significant* — unlikely to be chance — requires an inferential test, not a bigger decimal on the same mean.

## Statohub's Take on Fundamental Statistics

Statohub's position is that fundamental statistics is less a collection of formulas than a habit of asking three questions in order: what did I actually measure, how much does that number vary, and what am I entitled to conclude from it? Mean, variance, standard deviation, and z-score are the arithmetic that answers the second question; the descriptive/inferential split is what keeps the third question honest. Learn those pieces well and the rest of the field — distributions, regression, hypothesis testing — reads as variations on the same few moves, not a wall of unrelated techniques.
